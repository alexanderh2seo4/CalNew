# Pipeline training & engineering: modular (multi-stage) molecular property prediction for an odor model

Scope: how to train, connect, evaluate and deploy a 3-stage odor pipeline — (A) quality / multi-label descriptors (~5k molecules, GoodScents+Leffingwell style, ~138 labels), (B) potency (detection thresholds ~1–2k, noisy ~0.7 log units, partly censored; ordinal strength ~2.4k; dose–response intensity ~500), (C) combiner (perceived profile at a concentration; mixtures) — under small, heterogeneous, barely-overlapping datasets.

Research date: 2026-09-27. Everything below that is not attached to a URL is explicitly flagged as background knowledge or moved to Gaps. No metric is reported unless it appears in a cited source.

---

## Q1. Training strategies: separate + frozen stacking vs joint end-to-end vs multitask with shared encoder

### Takeaway
The strongest directly-relevant evidence (a 2026 paper doing exactly the vapor-pressure + odor-threshold multitask combination) is that naive joint multitask on small, imbalanced chemistry datasets causes measurable **negative transfer**, and that a "safe multitask" recipe — delayed auxiliary-task activation, tiny warmed-up loss weight, optional gradient detachment, per-task checkpoints, masked losses for missing labels — converts the auxiliary task into a mild regularizer rather than a co-driver. For an odor pipeline where stage B has ~1/5 the molecules of stage A, that argues for **separate training + frozen stage-A encoder as stage-B input**, with joint fine-tuning treated as an optional, carefully-guarded ablation rather than the default.

### Cited Findings
- A 2026 study trained a shared graph encoder (GINE or PNA) with separate linear heads for vapor pressure (VP; 1,852 molecules, 9,260 temperature-dependent records, PUFFIN) and odor detection threshold (OP; 1,042 molecules, 598 air / 444 water records) — i.e. almost exactly the stage-B data regime in question — [Safe Multitask Molecular Graph Networks for Vapor Pressure and Odor Threshold Prediction (arXiv 2601.16426)](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- Naive multitask training **degraded** the primary task: VP normalized MSE went from 0.210 (single-task) to 0.224 (naive MT); the paper attributes this to "negative transfer [that] arises when gradients from one task impede another, especially under task imbalance" — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- The "safe-MT" variant recovered and slightly improved on single-task VP (MSE 0.208 ± 0.004, MAE 0.341, R² = 0.904 with PNA; back-transformed VP RMSE 8.45×10³ Pa safe-MT vs 8.52×10³ Pa single-task). The auxiliary signal is described as "a mild regularizer rather than a co-driver" — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- Notably, for the *small, noisy* task (OP) the paper's best reported numbers are the **single-task** PNA (MSE 0.605 ± 0.017, MAE 0.608) — i.e. multitasking helped the data-rich task, not the data-poor one — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- The four safe-MT ingredients, explicitly: (1) delayed activation — auxiliary OP task only switched on at epoch 30 after VP stabilizes; (2) linear warmup of the auxiliary weight over 90 epochs to a final λ = 10⁻³, via λ_eff = λ · min(1, (e−e₀)/E_warm); (3) optional gradient detachment so OP gradients do not modify the shared backbone; (4) per-task checkpointing so each endpoint keeps its own best model — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- Missing-label handling in the same work is a plain **masked mean**: a binary mask m ∈ {0,1} per molecule/task, with ℒ_OP = (1/Σmᵢ) Σ mᵢ · ℓ(ŷᵢ, yᵢ), excluding unlabeled samples from the denominator — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- Gradient-conflict methods in the general MTL literature: PCGrad projects conflicting task gradients onto the normal plane of each other ([Gradient Surgery for Multi-Task Learning, NeurIPS 2020](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/file/3fe78a8acf5fda99de95303940a2420c-Paper.pdf)); GradNorm adaptively rescales task loss magnitudes to equalize training rates ([GradNorm, arXiv 1711.02257](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.02257))
- Reported comparative behaviour: "PCGrad outperforms GradNorm, indicating the importance of modifying both gradient directions and magnitudes"; separately, "uncertainty weighting tended to increase loss weights for certain tasks, while GradNorm was more aggressive in decreasing them; uncertainty weighting sometimes performed poorly on classification compared to GradNorm" — summarized across [Gradient Surgery (PCGrad)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.06782) and [Multi-Loss Weighting with Coefficient of Variations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.01717). Treat this as a cross-domain (mostly vision/RL) result, **not** chemistry-validated.
- Large-scale multitask molecular precedent: ADMET-AI trains Chemprop-RDKit models across the 41 ADMET datasets of TDC, with the multitask setting used for the classification and regression groups — [ADMET-AI, Bioinformatics 40(7):btae416 (2024)](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/7/btae416/7698030)

### Inferences
- **Default recommendation for this pipeline: staged training with a frozen stage-A encoder.** Stage A has the most molecules (~5k) and the densest supervision (138 labels); it is the natural representation learner. Stages B and C then consume (i) the frozen stage-A graph embedding and (ii) the stage-A predicted descriptor vector as features. This sidesteps the gradient-conflict problem entirely and matches the observed asymmetry in arXiv 2601.16426 (small noisy task did not benefit from sharing).
- **If you do try joint training**, port the safe-MT recipe directly: it is the only recipe in the literature validated on an odor-threshold task. Concretely — pretrain on stage A alone; introduce stage-B heads after stage-A validation plateaus; warm the stage-B weight up from 0 to a small λ; keep a `detach()` switch on the encoder input to the stage-B head; checkpoint per-head.
- **PCGrad/GradNorm/Kendall-style uncertainty weighting are unlikely to be the highest-leverage knob here.** With 3 heads and a ~5:2:1 data imbalance, the scheduling/weighting tricks above are cheaper and have direct in-domain evidence; gradient surgery costs a per-task backward pass and has, to my knowledge, no published win on small-data odor QSAR. Treat it as an ablation, not a foundation.
- Multitasking *within* stage A (138 descriptors as 138 heads on one encoder) is a different and much safer proposition than multitasking *across* stages A/B/C — the labels are co-measured on the same molecules and the imbalance is between labels, not datasets.

### Gaps
- I did **not** find a published head-to-head on an odor pipeline of "frozen stacking vs joint fine-tuning vs shared-encoder multitask" — the safe-MT paper only compares single-task, naive MT and safe MT on two tasks.
- I could not source a systematic "multitask learning helps or hurts small-data QSAR" benchmark from ChEMBL/ADMET within this session's search budget. The classic claims (Ramsundar et al. on massively multitask networks; the MoleculeNet-era finding that multitask helps mainly when tasks are correlated and data is sparse) are background knowledge to me and are **not sourced here** — the report writer should not present them as verified.
- No source found quantifying how much of PCGrad/GradNorm's benefit survives in molecular graph settings specifically.

## Q2. Stacking leakage: out-of-fold meta-features, stage-A outputs as stage-B inputs, scaffold-aware CV

### Takeaway
Feeding stage-A predictions into stage B is textbook stacking, and the textbook failure mode applies: if stage-A predicted descriptors for a molecule were produced by a stage-A model that saw that molecule's labels, stage B trains on optimistically-clean meta-features and is miscalibrated at deployment. The fix is out-of-fold (OOF) generation of stage-A meta-features, nested inside a **scaffold-aware** outer split, with the same scaffold grouping used in both the stage-A inner folds and the outer evaluation.

### Cited Findings
- "Theoretically, you have leakage if you train a second-level model on the same training set used to derive first-stage predictions"; the standard remedy is per-fold OOF prediction — for each fold and architecture, a model is trained on the union of the other folds and predicts on the held-out fold — [Leak-Free Cross-Validated Stacking with Per-Architecture Calibration (arXiv 2607.25367)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25367); [Kaggle: CV strategy when blending/stacking](https://www.kaggle.com/general/18793)
- Nested CV is the general leakage fix for any decision made on data: "the estimate is honest only when every model decision is made strictly on the training partition… nested cross-validation closes leakage by introducing a second inner loop within each training partition, where hyperparameters are selected entirely on outer-fold training data"; leakage arises when "feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, or imputation use the entire dataset" — [RobustModelMaker (arXiv 2606.01566)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.01566)
- Chemistry-specific split: scaffold-based splitting groups molecules by Bemis–Murcko scaffold; "novel scaffold holdout withholding structurally dissimilar scaffolds tests whether predictions transfer to chemistry the model has never seen" — [RobustModelMaker (arXiv 2606.01566)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.01566)
- The safe-MT odor/VP paper evaluates on scaffold splits at 80/10/10 across three independent splits, and additionally stratifies test error by max-ECFP4 Tanimoto similarity to training (median ≈ 0.4), reporting that "errors increase monotonically with decreasing molecular similarity to training set" — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- A stricter-than-scaffold alternative used in the ordinal odor-strength work: structurally similar compounds at ≥80% Tanimoto similarity were **grouped together** before splitting, to prevent leakage, on top of a stratified 80/20 split with 10-fold CV × 10 repetitions — [Machine learning for smell: ordinal odor strength prediction, RSC Adv.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- The odor reliability audit similarly uses scaffold-based splits so that "test molecules [are] structurally distinct from training set, preventing data leakage", with per-descriptor AUROC — [Beyond Predictive Accuracy: A Reliability-Aware Audit of Molecular Representations for Human Olfaction (arXiv 2607.24848)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24848)
- Step-forward / out-of-distribution CV is an established alternative to random CV for bioactivity, motivated by the fact that random CV overstates prospective performance — [Step Forward Cross Validation for Bioactivity Prediction (PMC11245006)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11245006/)

### Inferences
- **Concrete protocol.** Define one global molecule-level grouping (Bemis–Murcko scaffold, or Butina/Tanimoto clusters at ≥0.8 as in the RSC Adv. odor-strength work) over the *union* of A ∪ B ∪ C molecule sets. Assign every molecule a group id once, store it in the featurization cache, and use it for every split in the pipeline. This is the single most important engineering decision: a molecule that appears in both stage A and stage B must never be in stage-A-train and stage-B-test with a different group.
- **Stage-A meta-features must be OOF.** For the stage-B training molecules that also carry stage-A labels, generate their predicted-descriptor vector from a stage-A model that excluded their *group*. For stage-B molecules with no stage-A label, a stage-A model trained on all of A is fine (they were never in A) — but for consistency of the feature distribution it is safer to use the same k-fold ensemble average everywhere, and to note that OOF predictions are systematically slightly worse/wider than full-fit predictions, which is exactly the distribution you want stage B to learn on.
- **Two distinct meta-feature choices, and they leak differently.** (i) Frozen *embedding* from the stage-A encoder: leakage risk is via representation memorization, lower but non-zero, still needs group-consistent folds. (ii) Predicted *descriptor probabilities* (138-dim): leakage risk is direct and high, must be OOF. Evaluate both; the 138-d probability vector is the interpretable one ("this is a woody-musky molecule, hence high potency"), the embedding is usually the stronger one.
- **Do not tune stage-B hyperparameters against the same outer test fold used to report end-to-end numbers.** With n≈1k in stage B, the variance of a single 10% test fold is large; report mean ± sd over ≥3 independent scaffold splits as the safe-MT paper does, not a single split.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that specifically studies leakage *in multi-stage molecular pipelines* (i.e. quantifying the optimism from non-OOF stage-1 features in a chemistry stack). The recommendations above are the general stacking rules transferred to the chemistry split regime.

## Q3. Loss functions per stage

### Takeaway
Each stage has a well-matched loss with published chemistry precedent: masked/weighted BCE for stage-A multi-label imbalance; **bounded/censored (Tobit-style) regression** for detection thresholds, which Chemprop supports natively as "bounded MSE"; CORAL/CORN or cost-sensitive macro-MSE for ordinal strength; explicit **Hill-equation parameter** targets for dose–response; and a mixture-combination rule (Primacy model) rather than a free-form mixture loss.

### Cited Findings

**Stage A — multi-label descriptors**
- OpenPOM (DeepChem-integrated replication of the Principal Odor Map) trains on `curated_GS_LF_merged_4983.csv` = 4,983 molecules with odor labels, using 5-fold CV with an ensemble of 10 models per fold, reporting ROC-AUC 0.8872 — [ARY2260/openpom README](https://github.com/ARY2260/openpom/blob/main/README.md)
- The OpenPOM README does **not** state the loss function or license; those must be read from the code / the Lee et al. (2023) Science paper — [ARY2260/openpom README](https://github.com/ARY2260/openpom/blob/main/README.md)
- Multi-label odor prediction is characterized by "an exponentially large output space, dependence among labels, and often severe label imbalance" — [Conformal prediction for multi-label learning: a review (PubMed 42656157)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42656157/)

**Stage B — censored thresholds**
- Chemprop supports regression training with MSE, **bounded MSE (which allows inequalities as targets)**, or NLL under MVE/evidential distributions — [Chemprop, JCIM 64(1):9 (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10777403/)
- "Censored labels provide thresholds rather than precise values… standard approaches that quantify uncertainty in machine learning cannot fully utilize censored labels"; the cited remedy adapts ensemble-based, Bayesian and Gaussian models to learn from censored labels **using the Tobit model from survival analysis**, with the stated goal of improving the reliability of uncertainty estimates, not just point accuracy — [Enhancing uncertainty quantification in drug discovery with censored regression labels, Artificial Intelligence in the Life Sciences (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667318525000042) *(abstract-level only; full text returned HTTP 403 in this session)*
- For noisy threshold labels specifically, the safe-MT paper's concrete preprocessing + loss choices were: winsorization at the 2–2.5% quantiles per medium (air/water), **Huber robust loss with δ = 1.5 instead of MSE**, log-space median aggregation when a molecule has multiple reported thresholds, and an optional uncertainty-aware weighting using inter-quartile-range metadata — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)

**Stage B — ordinal strength**
- The odor-strength dataset: 2,393 molecules from Good Scents (1,678) + PubChem (715), four ordinal classes (odorless / low / medium / high), inter-annotator agreement Cohen's κ = 0.83, Krippendorff's α = 0.81 — [RSC Adv. odor strength (PMC13182554)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- Their loss design: "cost-sensitive learning via odor strength category weighted loss functions" for class imbalance, optimized against **macro-averaged MSE**, which "penalizes larger errors more heavily while equally weighting all odor strength categories"; models compared included CORAL (ordinal-specific MLP) alongside logistic regression, RF, XGBoost and MLP — [PMC13182554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- Reported: the direct ensemble achieved **72% classification accuracy**; SHAP attributed strength mainly to molecular weight, size, shape and polarity — [PMC13182554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- CORAL enforces rank monotonicity via a weight-sharing constraint in the output layer ([CORAL, arXiv 1901.07884](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07884)); CORN drops that weight-sharing constraint — which "may restrict the expressiveness and capacity of a network" — and instead obtains unconditional rank probabilities by chaining conditional probabilities over conditional training subsets, reported to improve substantially over CORAL and to be architecture-agnostic — [CORN, arXiv 2111.08851](https://arxiv.org/html/2111.08851v5); reference implementation [coral-pytorch](https://github.com/Raschka-research-group/coral-pytorch)

**Stage C — dose–response and mixtures**
- Dose–response is modeled with the **three-parameter Hill equation**, I = I_max · Cⁿ / (Cⁿ + EC50ⁿ), and the Hill form "outperformed alternative psychophysical functions (Stevens' power law, Beidler, Fechner)" — [A quantitative framework for predicting odor intensity across molecules and mixtures (PMC12363845)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)
- Their data scale: 62 odorants at ≥7 concentrations each (434 monomolecular points), plus 260 mixture samples (216 binary, 20 ternary, 16 five-component, 8 ten-component), ≥15 trained panelists per session, test–retest r = 0.97 — [PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)
- Architecture: a 5-layer DNN over 127 physicochemical descriptors (batch norm + ReLU); a reduced 10-feature variant using transport-related properties (MW, vapor pressure, boiling point, lipophilicity) performed comparably; vapor pressure was the single most important feature by permutation (p < 10⁻⁵) — [PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)
- **Mixture combination rule**: the *Primacy model* performed best (r = 0.85, RMSE = 8.6), estimating mixture intensity as "a weighted average using the intensities of individual components at the 20% concentration", motivated by early receptor activation during the first inhalation; the end-to-end NN on mixtures gave RMSE = 7.5, single-molecule prediction gave RMSE = 7.1, r = 0.87, versus shuffled controls at RMSE 15.8–20.4 — [PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)
- Mixture *quality* (not intensity) has its own line of work: GNNs predicting olfactory qualities of aroma-chemical blends, with architecture choices materially affecting performance — [Deep Learning for Odor Prediction on Aroma-Chemical Blends, ACS Omega (PMC11904650)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11904650/)
- In the DREAM Olfactory Mixtures Prediction Challenge, blend-level embeddings from a GIN-GNN fed into an XGBoost head produced competition-winning results — reported in [PMC11904650](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11904650/) *(exact winning-team attribution not verified here)*

### Inferences
- **Stage-B loss stack, concretely:** log10-transform thresholds; aggregate duplicate reports by log-space median; winsorize tails; train with a *censored* likelihood — Tobit NLL for left/right-censored records (">x", "<x") and Huber or Gaussian NLL for observed ones. Chemprop's bounded-MSE gives you the censored half for free without writing a custom loss; a custom Tobit NLL gives you censoring **and** a variance head in one, which matters for Q4.
- **Do not throw away censored rows and do not impute them at the censoring bound.** The whole point of the Tobit/bounded formulation is that ">10 ppb" is real information; imputing it as "10 ppb" biases the model toward the detection limit, which for odor thresholds is exactly where the interesting weak odorants live.
- **Ordinal strength and thresholds are different targets and should stay separate heads.** The RSC Adv. authors explicitly avoided mixing them (see Q6) — the ordinal labels are concentration-agnostic perfumery judgments, the thresholds are concentration-specific psychophysics.
- **Stage-A class imbalance:** with 138 descriptors over 5k molecules, most labels are rare. Per-label positive weighting in BCE (pos_weight = N_neg/N_pos, optionally capped) is the low-risk default; focal loss is the alternative but adds two hyperparameters (α, γ) you will be tuning against a noisy per-label AUROC. Note the odor-strength authors chose plain class-weighted losses over focal-style alternatives. I found **no** odor-specific evidence that focal loss beats weighted BCE — treat focal as an ablation.
- **Stage C should be parameter regression, not curve-point regression.** Predict (I_max, log EC50, n) per molecule and evaluate by reconstructing the curve; this makes the combiner analytic (you can evaluate intensity at any concentration) and makes the link to stage B explicit — log EC50 is essentially a suprathreshold analogue of the detection threshold, so stage-B outputs are the natural prior/feature for the EC50 head.
- The Primacy rule is a **strong, cheap baseline for the combiner** and should be the thing your learned combiner has to beat: component intensities at a normalized concentration → weighted average → mixture intensity.

### Gaps
- No source found for a validated **mixture-specific loss function** (as opposed to a mixture-combination rule + standard regression loss). Set-level losses (permutation-invariant, e.g. DeepSets/attention over components) are an architectural choice; I found no odor-specific benchmark of them in this session.
- The exact loss used by OpenPOM / the Principal Odor Map (I believe a class-imbalance-weighted BCE) is **not confirmed** by any source I could read — flagged rather than asserted.
- Licensing of the GoodScents/Leffingwell merged 4,983-molecule file is not stated in the OpenPOM README; the "research-use" assumption in the brief is unverified here.

## Q4. Uncertainty propagation across stages

### Takeaway
Chemprop v2 ships the full per-stage UQ toolchain (MVE, evidential, deep ensembles, dropout, quantile regression, plus conformal calibrators and calibration evaluators), MAPIE v1 covers the sklearn-side regression intervals and multi-label risk control, and the natural cross-stage mechanism is Monte-Carlo: sample from each stage's predictive distribution, push samples forward, and read off the combiner's empirical distribution. The censored-label literature warns that naive UQ is *wrong* when stage-B labels are censored.

### Cited Findings
- Chemprop 2.x uncertainty estimators: regression — **MVE, ensemble, evidential (total / epistemic / aleatoric), dropout, quantile-regression**; classification — classification, classification-dirichlet, multiclass-dirichlet — [Chemprop 2.3.1 Uncertainty Quantification docs](https://chemprop.readthedocs.io/en/main/uncertainty.html)
- Chemprop calibrators: regression — **ZScaling, Zelikman interval, MVE weighting, conformal regression**; classification — **Platt, isotonic, conformal** — [Chemprop UQ docs](https://chemprop.readthedocs.io/en/main/uncertainty.html)
- Chemprop evaluators: NLL (regression), **calibration area, expected normalized error, Spearman correlation** (between predicted uncertainty and error), plus NLL and **conformal coverage** evaluators for classification — [Chemprop UQ docs](https://chemprop.readthedocs.io/en/main/uncertainty.html)
- The documented Chemprop workflow is: train an MPNN with MVE loss → fit Z-scaling calibration **on the validation set** → evaluate on test; calibrated uncertainties are reported to improve identification of confident predictions — [Chemprop UQ docs](https://chemprop.readthedocs.io/en/main/uncertainty.html)
- Chemprop's own paper lists deep ensembles, dropout, MVE and evidential as the supported estimation methods — [Chemprop JCIM 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10777403/)
- Censoring breaks standard UQ: "standard approaches that quantify uncertainty in machine learning cannot fully utilize censored labels", and the fix is Tobit-adapted ensembles / Bayesian / Gaussian models — [Artificial Intelligence in the Life Sciences (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2667318525000042)
- MAPIE v1 (released 2025) computes prediction intervals (regression, time series) and prediction sets (classification) using conformal methods — [MAPIE docs](https://mapie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/); [MAPIE GitHub](https://github.com/scikit-learn-contrib/MAPIE)
- For **multi-label**, MAPIE implements three risk-control methods — **RCPS (Risk-Controlling Prediction Sets), CRC (Conformal Risk Control), and LTT (Learn Then Test)** — giving a guarantee on **recall** for any risk level in (0,1) — [MAPIE multilabel theory doc](https://github.com/scikit-learn-contrib/MAPIE/blob/v0.9.0/doc/theoretical_description_multilabel_classification.rst)
- Olfaction-specific reliability work applies conformal prediction ("non-parametric approach providing coverage guarantees without distributional assumptions") plus calibration, and finds "substantial variability in prediction reliability across different odor descriptors", with confidence-based filtering improving practical utility — [arXiv 2607.24848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24848)
- The safe-MT odor/VP paper reports seed-level standard deviations (5 seeds) but explicitly implements **no predictive uncertainty calibration** — a stated limitation of current odor-threshold modeling — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)

### Inferences
- **Cross-stage MC propagation, concretely:** for a query molecule, draw S samples (S ≈ 200–1000) from stage-A's per-descriptor predictive distribution (ensemble members × Dirichlet/Platt-calibrated probabilities) and from stage-B's predictive distribution (MVE/evidential mean+variance, or Tobit posterior), feed each sample pair through stage C, and report the combiner's empirical quantiles. This is the only mechanism that handles the fact that stage C is nonlinear (Hill curve) — a variance propagated by the delta method through a sigmoid near EC50 will be badly wrong.
- **Ensembles are the pragmatic default at this data scale.** MVE/evidential are single-model and cheap, but at n≈1k with 0.7-log-unit label noise the epistemic component dominates and ensembles (5–10 members, different seeds *and* different scaffold folds) are the most robust. Chemprop supports all of them, so this is a config flag, not a rewrite. Note the evidential literature's known caveat that evidential epistemic uncertainty can be poorly calibrated — I could not verify a specific citation for that in this session, so treat it as a hypothesis to test with Chemprop's `calibration area` / `expected normalized error` evaluators rather than an established fact.
- **Conformal is the right outer wrapper because it is distribution-free and composable per stage**, but be explicit that conformalizing each stage separately does **not** give you an end-to-end coverage guarantee for the combiner. If you need an honest end-to-end interval, conformalize the *final* quantity (perceived intensity at concentration c) on a held-out calibration set of end-to-end labels — e.g. Keller-style ratings — using the MC-propagated point prediction as the base predictor. This is the clean, defensible design.
- **Multi-label stage A should use risk control, not per-label intervals.** MAPIE's CRC/RCPS give you a guaranteed-recall descriptor set ("the true odor descriptors are in this set with recall ≥ 0.9"), which is far more useful to a perfumer than 138 independent calibrated probabilities.
- **Calibration data budget is the binding constraint.** Each conformal/calibration step consumes a held-out set. With stage B at ~1k molecules you cannot afford separate train/val/calibration/test splits at 10% each; use cross-conformal / CV+ (MAPIE supports CV-based conformal) rather than split conformal for stage B and C.

### Gaps
- No source found that does end-to-end uncertainty propagation across a multi-stage *molecular* pipeline with a coverage guarantee. The MC recipe above is standard practice, not a cited result.
- I could not verify within budget whether MAPIE v1's regression API integrates cleanly with a Chemprop model as a `sklearn`-style estimator (it requires a wrapper exposing `fit`/`predict`); flagged as an engineering unknown.

## Q5. Tooling in 2026, code structure, Apple Silicon

### Takeaway
Chemprop v2 is the center of gravity — it covers multitask MPNN training, bounded/censored targets, MVE/evidential/ensemble UQ and conformal calibration in one CLI — with DeepChem/OpenPOM for the odor-specific stage-A replication, MAPIE for sklearn-side conformal, LightGBM/XGBoost for meta-learners over stage-A features, and Optuna + MLflow/W&B for the search and tracking. Structure the repo around a single shared featurization cache keyed by canonical SMILES + a frozen scaffold-group id.

### Cited Findings
- Chemprop v2 documentation is live at [chemprop.readthedocs.io](https://chemprop.readthedocs.io/en/main/uncertainty.html) (2.3.1 at time of access; 2.2.x branches also published), with uncertainty estimator/calibrator/evaluator classes exposed in the public API — [chemprop.uncertainty.estimator API](https://chemprop.readthedocs.io/en/main/autoapi/chemprop/uncertainty/estimator/index.html)
- Chemprop's package paper describes it as covering multitask prediction and the UQ methods above — [Chemprop JCIM 64(1):9 (2024)](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jcim.3c01250)
- OpenPOM: "implemented such that it integrates with DeepChem"; ships an ensemble of 10 MPNN models trained on the POM dataset, a SMILES-input demo GUI producing a top-N odor-term barplot, and one-click TXT/JSON export; available on PyPI — [ARY2260/openpom](https://github.com/ARY2260/openpom); [openpom on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/openpom/); a second fork at [BioMachineLearning/openpom](https://github.com/BioMachineLearning/openpom)
- ADMET-AI is distributed both as a web server (admet.ai.greenstonebio.com) and as an open-source Python package for local batch prediction ([github.com/swansonk14/admet_ai](https://github.com/swansonk14/admet_ai)), with data and models archived on [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/10372419) — [ADMET-AI, Bioinformatics 2024](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/7/btae416/7698030)
- ADMET-AI is reported as the fastest web-based ADMET predictor, with a **45% reduction in time compared to the next fastest public ADMET web server** — [ADMET-AI, Bioinformatics 2024](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/7/btae416/7698030)
- MAPIE is the scikit-learn-contrib conformal library; v1 (2025) changed the API substantially — [MAPIE GitHub](https://github.com/scikit-learn-contrib/MAPIE); [MAPIE v1 announcement](https://medium.com/capgemini-invent-lab/uncertainty-quantified-mapie-v1-is-here-b0d0f953b5ac)
- Molecular representations available as drop-in featurizers in 2026 odor work: RDKit descriptors (217 parameters), Morgan / MACCS / topological fingerprints, and pretrained neural encoders **ChemBERTa-2, ChemProp, CheMeleon** (CheMeleon pretrained on 77M molecules) — notably, "no dramatic performance advantages emerged across descriptor–predictor combinations" — [PMC13182554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- Qptuna is an existing automated Optuna-based QSAR modelling platform, i.e. Optuna-driven QSAR search is an established pattern — [Qptuna preprint](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/65b8e0209138d231610940c9/original/qptuna-an-automated-qsar-modelling-platform-for-molecular-property-prediction-in-drug-design.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Repo layout that makes the multi-stage design tractable:**
  - `data/` raw per-source tables; `data/registry.parquet` = one row per canonical SMILES with InChIKey, Bemis–Murcko scaffold, cluster id, and boolean membership flags for A/B-threshold/B-ordinal/B-doseresponse/C. This registry is what makes masked losses and group-consistent splits possible at all.
  - `features/cache/` keyed by InChIKey → {RDKit descriptors, Morgan bits, stage-A embedding, stage-A OOF descriptor probabilities, fold id}. Compute once; every stage reads it. RDKit featurization dominates CPU time and is trivially cacheable.
  - `stages/a_quality/`, `stages/b_potency/`, `stages/c_combiner/` each with `train.py`, `predict.py`, `config.yaml`; a top-level `pipeline.py` that only wires artifacts, never re-featurizes.
  - Stage artifacts pinned by hash (model weights + the fold id they were trained under) so that OOF provenance is auditable — this is the practical defense against the Q2 leakage failure.
- **Meta-learner choice:** LightGBM/XGBoost over [stage-A probabilities ⊕ stage-A embedding ⊕ RDKit descriptors] is the right stage-B/C head at n≈1k–2k. The RSC Adv. odor-strength result — XGBoost competitive with everything else and no representation dominating — supports not over-engineering here.
- **Apple Silicon (M-series):** PyTorch MPS works for these model sizes and the whole pipeline (5k molecules, small MPNNs) is comfortably laptop-scale; the practical caveats I would verify before relying on them are (a) some scatter/segment ops used by PyG/Chemprop message passing have historically fallen back to CPU on MPS, and (b) `torch.float64` is not supported on MPS. Both are **background knowledge, unverified in this session** — validate with a single-epoch smoke test and `PYTORCH_ENABLE_MPS_FALLBACK=1`. RDKit and LightGBM/XGBoost have native arm64 wheels. Given the data scale, CPU-only training is a legitimate fallback and removes the whole class of MPS issues.
- **Tracking:** with ≥3 scaffold splits × 5 seeds × 3 stages × ablations, you are in the hundreds-of-runs regime; MLflow or W&B is not optional bookkeeping, it *is* the evaluation apparatus. Log the fold id and the registry hash with every run.

### Gaps
- I did not verify current Chemprop v2 support for **ordinal** heads (CORAL/CORN) — as documented it exposes regression / binary / multiclass / multilabel, so the ordinal stage likely needs a custom head or a separate `coral-pytorch` model.
- No 2026-current source found benchmarking Apple Silicon MPS vs CUDA for Chemprop/PyG specifically.
- I did not confirm whether OpenPOM is actively maintained in 2026 (two forks exist; release recency not checked).

## Q6. Evaluation design: per-stage metrics, end-to-end, ablations, baselines

### Takeaway
Per-stage metrics are well-established in the cited odor literature (per-descriptor AUROC for A; log-unit RMSE/MAE + similarity-stratified error for B-thresholds; macro-MSE/accuracy for B-ordinal; curve RMSE + correlation for C). The end-to-end target is Keller/DREAM-style: perceived intensity and pleasantness plus semantic descriptors **at two concentrations**, which is exactly the "profile at concentration" the combiner is supposed to produce. The most informative ablation in the literature is error stratified by Tanimoto similarity to training.

### Cited Findings
- The DREAM Olfaction Prediction Challenge data: perceptual ratings of **476 molecules by 49 subjects at 2 different concentrations (high and low)**, with **21 perceptual attributes** — intensity, pleasantness, and 19 semantic descriptors; models had to predict per-individual ratings and the population mean and standard deviation — [Keller et al., Science 355:820 (2017)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aal2014); [Stanford-hosted PDF](https://www-cs.stanford.edu/people/marinka/papers/olfaction-science17.pdf)
- Reported outcome: models "accurately predicted odor intensity and pleasantness and also successfully predicted 8 among 19 rated semantic descriptors ('garlic,' 'fish,' 'sweet,' 'fruit,' 'burnt,' 'spices,' 'flower,' and 'sour')" — [Keller et al. 2017](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aal2014)
- Follow-on work reported improved personalized prediction from large-scale chemoinformatic features — [Accurate prediction of personalized olfactory perception, GigaScience 7(2):gix127](https://academic.oup.com/gigascience/article/7/2/gix127/4750780)
- The 2026 olfaction audit evaluates with **per-descriptor AUROC** under scaffold splits and argues that "accuracy metrics alone miss critical reliability gaps", recommending confidence-based filtering and reporting that reliability varies substantially by descriptor — [arXiv 2607.24848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24848)
- Similarity-stratified evaluation as a standard ablation: test error binned by max-ECFP4 Tanimoto to the training set, increasing monotonically as similarity falls (median ≈ 0.4) — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- Metric conventions in the cited works: normalized-space MSE/MAE/R² **plus** back-transformed physical units (e.g. VP RMSE in Pa) reported side by side — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426); mixture/intensity work reports **RMSE and Pearson r against shuffled-label controls** (RMSE 15.8–20.4 shuffled vs 7.1–8.6 real) — [PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)
- Ordinal-stage metrics: macro-averaged MSE as the optimization metric, classification accuracy as the headline (72% for the direct ensemble) — [PMC13182554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- Stage-A benchmark point of reference: OpenPOM reports ROC-AUC 0.8872 under 5-fold CV with 10-model ensembles — [ARY2260/openpom README](https://github.com/ARY2260/openpom/blob/main/README.md)
- An explicit warning against conflating the stage-B sub-targets: the odor-strength authors note that odor detection threshold "does not necessarily equal perceived intensity" and deliberately avoided mixing concentration-dependent psychophysical data with concentration-agnostic ordinal labels — [PMC13182554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- Independently, the intensity-framework authors state that "suprathreshold intensity cannot reliably be inferred from detection thresholds alone", and that detection thresholds "show high variability across studies and odors" — [PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)

### Inferences
- **The two sources above jointly constrain the architecture, and this is probably the single most important finding for the project.** Two independent 2025–2026 papers say that threshold → suprathreshold intensity is not a reliable inference. A pipeline whose stage C computes perceived intensity from a predicted detection threshold alone is therefore building on a link the literature says is weak. The defensible design is: stage B predicts *multiple* potency views (threshold, ordinal strength, Hill EC50/I_max/n), and stage C learns the mapping from all of them plus volatility descriptors — with an explicit ablation showing how much each view contributes.
- **Report shuffled-label controls.** The intensity paper's shuffled-control RMSEs are the cheapest possible guard against "my end-to-end pipeline looks good because intensity has low variance". Do the same at every stage.
- **Ablation grid worth running:** (1) remove stage A entirely (stage B/C on raw features only) — tests whether the quality model carries any potency signal; (2) stage-A embedding only vs stage-A predicted-descriptors only vs both; (3) remove each stage-B sub-target from the combiner; (4) replace the learned combiner with the analytic Primacy rule; (5) OOF vs non-OOF meta-features — the delta is your leakage magnitude, and it is worth reporting because it is rarely quantified; (6) single end-to-end model (one MPNN, all targets, masked multitask) as the flat baseline the modular pipeline must beat.
- **The single end-to-end baseline is essential and is currently missing from the odor literature I found.** Without it, modularity is an assumption, not a result. Note that the safe-MT paper's naive-MT row is effectively a two-task version of this baseline and it *lost*, which is weak evidence in the modular pipeline's favour.
- **Metric list, per stage:** A — per-descriptor AUROC + AUPRC (AUPRC matters at 138 imbalanced labels), macro and micro, plus calibration area; B-threshold — RMSE/MAE in log10 units, % within 3× and 10× (i.e. within 0.48 and 1.0 log units), Spearman, with censored records scored by a censoring-aware metric (a prediction above a ">x" bound should not be penalized); B-ordinal — macro-MSE, accuracy, QWK; C — Hill-parameter RMSE, reconstructed-curve RMSE, and intensity-at-concentration RMSE/r; end-to-end — Keller-style held-out molecules, per-attribute Pearson r at each of the two concentrations.
- **Noise ceiling.** With ~0.7 log-unit label noise on thresholds, an RMSE near 0.7 log units is approximately the irreducible floor; report the ceiling alongside the metric so that 0.75 is not read as failure. (The 0.7 figure comes from the assignment brief, not from a source I verified.)

### Gaps
- I did not find a published end-to-end evaluation of a *modular* odor pipeline on Keller 2016/2017 held-out data — i.e. the exact experiment proposed here appears to be unpublished. That is an opportunity, but it also means there is no reference number to target.
- % within 3×/10× is not a metric I found used in the odor-threshold literature (it is standard in PK/solubility work); flagged as a sensible import, not an established odor convention.
- The DREAM Olfactory Mixtures Prediction Challenge results are referenced second-hand via ACS Omega; I did not reach a primary source for its metrics or leaderboard.

## Q7. Case studies of modular pipelines in molecular ML, and lessons

### Takeaway
ADMET-AI is the best-documented "many small heterogeneous datasets, one deployed system" case study and its lesson is about *contextualization and throughput* more than about clever coupling: it trains Chemprop-RDKit models over the TDC ADMET suite and adds a reference-distribution layer (comparison to DrugBank approved drugs, filterable by ATC code) so raw predictions become actionable. The safe-MT odor/VP paper is the best case study of what goes wrong when stages are coupled carelessly.

### Cited Findings
- ADMET-AI's stated motivation: large chemical repositories, combinatorial spaces, high-throughput docking and generative AI have expanded candidate sets, so selection requires filtering on ADMET properties — [ADMET-AI, Bioinformatics 2024](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/7/btae416/7698030)
- ADMET-AI's distinguishing design feature: it "uniquely provides context by comparing ADMET predictions on input molecules to predictions on approved drugs from the DrugBank, which can optionally be filtered to a specific drug category by ATC code" — [ADMET-AI, Bioinformatics 2024](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/7/btae416/7698030)
- ADMET-AI is deployed in dual form (web server + local batch Python package) with models/data archived — [ADMET-AI](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/7/btae416/7698030); [Zenodo record](https://zenodo.org/records/10372419)
- OpenPOM demonstrates the deployment pattern for the odor stage specifically: ensemble of 10 MPNNs + SMILES-in GUI + top-N descriptor barplot + TXT/JSON export — [ARY2260/openpom](https://github.com/ARY2260/openpom)
- Modular composition in the mixtures domain: GIN-GNN blend embeddings → XGBoost head won the DREAM Olfactory Mixtures challenge — i.e. a frozen-representation + gradient-boosted-meta-learner stack, which is precisely the stage-A→stage-C pattern proposed here — [PMC11904650](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11904650/)
- Lesson from the coupled-stages failure case: per-task checkpointing was necessary because the two endpoints do not reach their optima at the same epoch; and the auxiliary task had to be both delayed and down-weighted (λ = 10⁻³) to avoid degrading the primary one — [arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)
- Lesson on representation choice: across seven encodings (RDKit descriptors, Morgan/MACCS/topological FPs, ChemBERTa-2, ChemProp, CheMeleon) and five predictors, "no dramatic performance advantages emerged" for the odor-strength task — [PMC13182554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)
- Lesson on reliability: in olfaction specifically, reliability varies substantially **per descriptor**, and confidence-based filtering (rather than uniform trust) is what makes predictions practically usable — [arXiv 2607.24848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24848)

### Inferences
- **Transferable lesson 1 — ship the reference distribution, not just the number.** ADMET-AI's DrugBank-percentile framing is directly portable: an odor pipeline should report "predicted threshold is in the 15th percentile of known perfumery materials" alongside the raw ppb value. At 0.7-log-unit noise, a percentile is honest where a point estimate is not.
- **Transferable lesson 2 — frozen representation + boosted meta-learner is the validated composition pattern** in this exact domain (DREAM mixtures winner). This is corroborating evidence for the frozen-stacking default proposed in Q1.
- **Transferable lesson 3 — modularity buys auditability, which is the real deliverable.** Because each stage has its own metric, its own calibration set and its own failure mode (stage A: rare descriptors; stage B: censoring and 0.7-log noise; stage C: mixture nonlinearity), a modular pipeline lets you say *which* stage was responsible for a bad prediction. A single end-to-end model cannot.
- **Transferable lesson 4 — don't over-invest in the featurizer.** Two of the cited odor papers found representation choice roughly second-order; the wins came from loss design, split design and task scheduling. Budget accordingly.
- Retrosynthesis multi-stage pipelines (single-step model + search + in-scope filter) are the other classic modular case study, but I did not source them in this session — flagged so the report writer does not attribute unsupported claims.

### Gaps
- No source found for a "lessons learned" retrospective on multi-stage *PK* pipelines that compose predicted properties (e.g. predicted logP/solubility feeding a PK model) — this was in the brief and I could not verify it within budget.
- Retrosynthesis multi-stage pipeline citations were not retrieved.
- ADMET-AI's paper does not (in what I could access) report whether its multitask setup was compared against single-task per-endpoint models, so I cannot cite it as evidence for or against multitask in small-data chemistry.
