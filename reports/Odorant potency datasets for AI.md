# Licenses, not algorithms, cap odor-potency AI

Free odor-potency data is scarce, scattered and unevenly licensed: structure-resolved detection thresholds for about 1,000 molecules in water and a few hundred in air, rated intensity for roughly 500 molecules (mostly at just two dilutions), about 314 fitted dose–response curves, ~2,000 ordinal "odor strength" labels that must be re-scraped from a copyrighted website, and receptor EC50s for a few thousand active odorant–receptor pairs. The ready-to-train files are Hou et al. 2026 (1,003 water thresholds), Li et al. 2025 (716 water thresholds, CC BY 4.0 on Zenodo) and pyrfume's copy of Abraham 2012 (268 air thresholds), with Keller & Vosshall 2016 (480 molecules, CC BY 4.0) anchoring intensity and M2OR (Apache-2.0) covering receptors. The authoritative compilations (van Gemert, VCF, Devos, AIHA, Leibniz-LSB@TUM) are paid or forbid bulk extraction, and several popular MIT-licensed repositories wrap upstream data they cannot relicense, so a commercial model can rely cleanly only on a small core of CC BY, Apache, Etalab and US-government sources. Label noise is the second hard wall: the same compound's published threshold differs by two to four orders of magnitude between studies, and even single-lab repeats scatter by about 0.66 log units. A realistic target is therefore about 0.8 log units of error (R² ≈ 0.8) on random splits, with much weaker performance on unseen chemical scaffolds. Descriptors fed to gradient-boosted trees still match or beat pretrained foundation models on every published potency benchmark. Progress will come from better labels, disciplined harmonization, and volatility and receptor data used as auxiliary tasks, not from larger networks.

## Downloadable thresholds cover about 1,000 water and a few hundred air molecules

The odor detection threshold (ODT) is the most direct potency label: the lowest concentration a panel can tell apart from a blank. The literature holds only a limited number of them. The subscription VCF database lists odor thresholds for **1,789 compounds in air and 1,881 in water** ([VCF](https://www.vcf-online.nl/OFTVCompounds.cfm)). The van Gemert 2011 compilation holds about **22,000 values**, covering roughly 1,790 compounds in air and 1,860 in water ([Leffingwell & Associates](http://www.leffingwell.com/bacis2.htm)). Hou et al. put the number of compounds with experimentally determined thresholds at "only around 1800" ([Hou et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/)). Every free dataset is a subset of that pool, and most draw on the same few compilations.

### Three files are ready to train today

The largest downloadable set is **Hou et al. 2026 (npj Science of Food): 1,003 compounds with aqueous ODTs**. It was drawn mainly from van Gemert plus post-2011 literature, filtered with an explicit outlier rule, aggregated by geometric mean, and released in an MIT-licensed GitHub repository ([PMC13558726](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/); [GitHub](https://github.com/Hvard24/odt-prediction-ml)). **Li et al. 2025 (Metabolites) reports 716 water-matrix ODTs.** They were harmonized to mg/L, aggregated by median, sourced from PubChem, Flavornet and the literature, and modeled as −log10 ([PMC12654368](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12654368/)). Its Zenodo deposits are CC BY 4.0 and include `threshold_data_med.csv` and the trained models ([Zenodo 17559514](https://zenodo.org/records/17559514); [Zenodo 17605258](https://zenodo.org/records/17605258)). The matching GitHub CSV, however, holds **992 rows and no LICENSE file**. Resolve that version mismatch before relying on the data ([GitHub](https://github.com/yuanhonglun/odor_prediction_models)).

Pyrfume's `abraham_2012` folder gives **268 PubChem CIDs with isomeric SMILES, log(1/ODT) in air, and Abraham solvation descriptors**. Its build script averages about 350 raw rows: 211 CIDs have one value, 37 have two, 15 have three and 5 have four ([behavior.csv](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/main/abraham_2012/behavior.csv); [main.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/main/abraham_2012/main.py)). The source paper placed 353 ODTs on a single "Nagata scale". 193 of them came from Nagata's triangle-bag measurements, and the rest were rescaled from three other labs ([Abraham et al. 2012](https://academic.oup.com/chemse/article/37/3/207/325917)).

### Government and web tables are free but need parsing

PubChem's "Odor Threshold" heading carries **819 annotations (433 from HSDB, 386 from Haz-Map) for 511 CIDs**. The values are free-text strings that mix air and water, detection and recognition, and low and high estimates. They use six unit families, led by ppm (684 items) and mg/cu m (242) ([PubChem PUG-View](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug_view/annotations/heading/JSON?heading=Odor%20Threshold)).

The Japanese Ministry of the Environment hosts **Nagata's 223 thresholds**, measured between 1976 and 1988 by a six-member trained panel using the triangle odor bag method. They range from **0.77 ppt (isoamyl mercaptan) to 1,500 ppm (propane)**. This is the most internally consistent free air dataset. The PDF gives only substance names, though, and its text layer separates names from values ([MoE PDF](https://www.env.go.jp/en/air/odor/measure/02_3_2.pdf)).

The **US EPA 1992 guide** screened published values for the 189 hazardous air pollutants against 13 quality criteria. It reports geometric means with CAS numbers, but the NEPIS file is an image-only scan that needs OCR ([NEPIS](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=2000BHG5.TXT)).

Leffingwell's 1999 web table lists **268 odor thresholds in ppb in water**. It identifies compounds only by FEMA number and name (no CAS), gives some values as ranges, carries no reuse licence, and is partly abstracted from the commercial FlavorBase ([Leffingwell](http://www.leffingwell.com/odor.htm)). The **AIHA 2013 second edition** covers 295 chemicals, but it gives min–max ranges rather than central values: acetaldehyde, for example, spans 0.0015–1,000 ppm. A full copy circulates online, but AIHA holds the copyright ([SWESIAQ-hosted PDF](https://swesiaq.se/onewebmedia/Dokument/ODOR%20THRESHOLDS.pdf)).

### The authoritative compilations are paid or permission-gated

The **Leibniz-LSB@TUM Odorant Database (v1.2) covers more than 1,700 odorants from over 700 publications**, with thresholds in several matrices measured under the Czerny 2008 protocol. It is free to browse, but searches return at most 20 hits. Its terms state that "systematic download" or use "in similar products" requires prior written permission ([Leibniz-LSB terms](https://www.leibniz-lsb.de/en/databases/leibniz-lsbtum-odorant-database/copyright-and-citation); [user guide](https://www.leibniz-lsb.de/en/databases/leibniz-lsbtum-odorant-database/user-guide)). A request to archive it in pyrfume has been open since 2020 ([pyrfume issue #50](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/issues/50)). Czerny et al. 2008 re-measured only **84 compounds**, not the thousand-plus sometimes attributed to it ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00217-008-0931-x)). Devos et al. 1990 standardized 529 substances ([INRS record](https://portaildocumentaire.inrs.fr/Default/doc/SYRACUSE/74533/standardized-human-olfactory-thresholds-seuils-olfactifs-normalises-chez-l-homme?_lg=fr-FR)), and Dunkel et al. 2014 defined 226 key food odorants ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24939725/)). Both are paywalled.

Several larger curated sets exist but have not been released. Wu, Wang & Yu assembled **1,042 molecules (598 air, 444 water)** by scraping public tables. They publish only numeric targets, citation pointers and re-download scripts, and no live repository could be verified ([arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426v1)). Sun, Jia & Xi trained on **1,741 compounds** with thresholds spanning 10⁻⁹ to 10⁸ mg/m³; whether that data will be released is unknown ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7070562)). Toropov's QSPR sets of 906 and 1,259 compounds also have no verified data file ([PubMed 27500544](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27500544/); [PubMed 29209954](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29209954/)). Hugging Face and Kaggle hold no molecular threshold dataset; their olfaction sets contain odor descriptors or electronic-nose sensor data ([Hugging Face search](https://huggingface.co/datasets?search=odor)).

The free sources overlap heavily. The industrial-hygiene air sources (EPA, AIHA, HSDB, Nagata) keep re-citing the same compilations. Inside PubChem's HSDB records, ASTM DS 48A is cited 152 times and Ruth 1986 145 times ([PUG-View](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug_view/annotations/heading/JSON?heading=Odor%20Threshold)). Those sources also concentrate on small solvents, sulfur compounds and amines. The food and flavor sources, measured in water, add pyrazines, thiols, lactones and furanones, so the two clusters complement each other. After merging and deduplication, I estimate **1,000–2,000 unique structures** across both media. This is an estimate, not a measured count: nobody has published a deduplicated union.

## Rated intensity reaches about 500 molecules, mostly at two dilutions

Thresholds describe the bottom of the dose–response curve. Suprathreshold intensity, meaning how strong an odor smells above threshold, is the second face of potency, and its free data is thinner still. **Keller & Vosshall 2016 is the anchor set: 480 molecules at two concentrations, rated by 55 subjects on a 0–100 "How strong is the smell?" slider.** The stimuli were diluted in paraffin oil. The authors made "the entire dataset freely available" under **CC BY 4.0** ([PMC4977894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4977894/)). The bioRxiv preprint was CC BY-NC, so cite and use the BMC version ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/049999v1.full.pdf)).

The design limits what the data can support. Dilutions are not matched across molecules, since 437 of the 1,000 stimuli sit at the same 1/1,000 liquid dilution. Many stimuli barely clear the blank: water alone scored 14.44, and the authors estimate that about 126 molecules are undetectable at 1/1,000. And two points cannot define a curve; the authors note that two dilutions "are not sufficient" to fix concentration–response functions ([PMC4977894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4977894/)).

Pyrfume's ready-made `prediction_targets/keller_2016.csv` averages "across all concentrations, replicates, and subjects". That strips out exactly the concentration information a potency model needs ([pyrfume manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/prediction_targets/manifest.toml)). The DREAM challenge subset (476 molecules, 49 subjects) lives in an MIT-licensed GitHub repo as tab-separated files. Its training file has 35,084 rows covering 338 CIDs ([dream-olfaction repo](https://github.com/dream-olfaction/olfaction-prediction)).

The next-best sets each give up something. **Lee et al. 2023 (POM) Data S3** has replicated per-subject intensity (0–10) for 398 samples from 18 raters, with a "NoOdor" flag and GC-O purity labels, but structures are public for only 208 of the samples ([osmoai/publications, Apache-2.0](https://github.com/osmoai/publications/tree/main/lee_et_al_2023/data)). Keller 2012 covers 66 odors at two concentrations rated by 391 people on a category scale, under CC BY ([PMC3493268](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3493268/)). Ravia 2020 offers 181 single molecules, 72 of them at three dilutions, with no stated licence ([pyrfume manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/ravia_2020/manifest.toml)). **Ma 2021 (INRAE)** has 72 food odorants and 222 binary mixtures rated by 30 trained assessors under the **Etalab 2.0** open licence ([Recherche Data Gouv](https://entrepot.recherche.data.gouv.fr/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.15454/51OVY6)), and a 2025 *Scientific Data* lay-panel set adds 74 odors rated by 1,227 participants plus an intensity pilot file under CC BY 4.0 ([Zenodo 15657278](https://zenodo.org/records/15657278)). SmellSpace has 24 molecules rated by 983 online participants, but only under CC BY-NC ([pyrfume manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/snitz_2019/manifest.toml)).

Dose–response data is where the few genuine curves live. **Wakayama et al. 2019 (Kao) published fitted intensity curves (I_max, C, D on the Labeled Magnitude Scale) for 314 perfumery raw materials.** It is the cleanest single-lab dose–response target available ([ACS](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.iecr.9b01225)). Pyrfume's copy, however, was "scraped from supplemental pdf" and carries no licence ([pyrfume manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/wakayama_2019/manifest.toml)).

The methodological gold standard is **Pellegrino et al. 2025: 62 odorants at seven or more concentrations each**, delivered in Nalophan bags to 36 panelists rated on the gLMS. It is too small for training, and no open repository was found ([PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)). The classic compilation of 110 Stevens power-law slopes (Patte 1975) exists only as a paywalled PDF ([Chemical Senses](https://academic.oup.com/chemse/article-abstract/1/3/283/401623)). The Dravnieks atlas presented all its samples at deliberately similar intensity, so it carries no intensity ratings at all ([ASTM DS61](https://store.astm.org/ds61-eb.html)).

Iso-intensity designs such as Ma, Ravia and Dravnieks store potency in the *concentration the experimenters chose*, not in the rating. For those datasets the model should predict the concentration.

### Ordinal strength and odorless labels trade quality for scale

The only large-N potency proxy is The Good Scents Company's ordinal field. A typical entry reads "Odor strength: high, recommend smelling in a 10.00 % solution or less", alongside a substantivity figure in hours ([TGSC benzaldehyde page](http://www.thegoodscentscompany.com/data/rw1001491.html)). The site states that "Permission to reproduce is required" ([TGSC copyright](http://www.thegoodscentscompany.com/misc/copyrigh.html)).

Fichtelmann & Westermayr crawled all 42,234 Good Scents entries and built **2,056 molecules in four classes** (odorless 383, low 244, medium 1,071, high 358). Only 13 compounds were labelled "very high", so they merged that class into "high". Some Good Scents SMILES were wrong, and they re-resolved those structures from CAS numbers. They release MIT-licensed scraping code and the PubChem-derived portion of the data on Zenodo, but not the Good Scents records themselves ([arXiv 2512.08683](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08683); [Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17828661)). Pyrfume's 2022 Good Scents scrape also contains 739 strength values and 1,305 substantivity values in its raw file. Its manifest points to a LICENSE file that does not exist ([pyrfume manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/goodscents/manifest.toml)).

Odorless labels add a censored boundary, meaning "threshold above the tested amount". **Mayhew et al. 2022 labelled 1,924 molecules (1,615 odorous, 309 odorless).** Their lab QC found that **22% of molecules classified as odorous were actually odorless compounds contaminated with odorous impurities** ([PMC9169660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169660/)). OlfactionBase adds 1,124 odorless compounds, but its data is "All Rights Reserved" ([NAR 2022](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/50/D1/D678/6362078)).

## Receptor EC50s and volatility data are plentiful but sit upstream of perception

**M2OR is the only large, openly downloadable odorant–receptor dataset with systematic potency values.** It holds **75,050 bioassay experiments covering 51,395 receptor–molecule pairs**, 768 molecules and 1,246 receptor sequences from 11 species. It records either an EC50 or a screening concentration for every experiment ([M2OR, NAR 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10767820/)). Only about **6% of pairs are agonists (roughly 3,100)**. The rest (48,295) are non-responders, and the number of pairs with a measured EC50 has not been published. Each non-response at a known screening concentration is a genuine lower bound on EC50, which makes censored-regression losses natural.

The GitHub snapshot (`M2OR_20230428.csv`) is **Apache-2.0**, but it lags the web portal ([GitHub](https://github.com/chemosim-lab/M2OR)). The companion status report puts median EC50 disagreement between repeated experiments at **0.54 log(M)**. That is the noise floor for any receptor model. Most EC50s sit in the micromolar-to-millimolar range, and 87% fall below a 500 µM screening concentration ([Chemical Senses 2024](https://dx.doi.org/10.1093/chemse/bjae037)).

The other receptor resources add little to potency modeling. Mainland 2015 screened 73 odorants against 511 human receptors, but its figshare licence and EC50 count are unverified ([PMC4412152](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4412152/)). Trimmer 2019's genotype-linked perception data sits behind dbGaP controlled access ([PMC6511007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6511007/)). DoOR is an insect dataset whose response units are normalized across labs, so it contains no molar potency ([Sci Rep 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep21841)). ChEMBL is CC BY-SA 3.0, and its share-alike clause complicates mixing it with differently licensed data ([ChEMBL licensing](http://chembl.github.io/chembl-licensing/)).

Receptor potency does track perception, but the evidence is narrow. For musk odorants, receptor EC50 correlated with median human detection threshold at **r = 0.87**, and efficacy at r = −0.91. EC20, the low foot of the dose–response curve, predicted the sensory threshold for OR5A2 better than EC50 did ([Current Biology 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982223003044)). There is also a large scale gap. Human receptors appear tuned around ~100 µM of odorant, while perceptual thresholds sit in the ppb-to-ppt range. Air–mucus partitioning and volatility bridge that gap, which is why physicochemical data is essential rather than optional.

Free physicochemical data, by contrast, is abundant. Sander's Henry's-law compilation holds **46,434 values for 10,173 species** ([ACP 2023](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/23/10901/2023/)). PHYSPROP has experimental vapor pressure, boiling point, log Kow and Henry's law constants for **more than 43,000 compounds** ([EPI Suite docs](https://episuite.dev/assets/help/ecosar/physprop_database.html)), and the open-source OPERA models fill gaps with applicability-domain flags ([OPERA](https://github.com/kmansouri/OPERA)). The UFZ-LSER database supplies Abraham solvation descriptors and suits small neutral odorants well ([UFZ-LSER](https://www.ufz.de/lserd/)). For vapor-pressure learning, PUFFIN gives 1,852 molecules with 9,260 temperature-dependent records ([arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)), while GeckoQ's 31,637 values are DFT-computed for atmospheric oxidation products, a poor chemical match for fragrance molecules ([Sci Data 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-023-02366-x)). Bulk-download terms for henrys-law.org and UFZ-LSER were not confirmed. Also track which values are experimental and which are predicted: PHYSPROP values feed OPERA, and OPERA predictions populate CompTox, so validating against CompTox values can become circular.

## Only a narrow core survives a commercial licence audit

The biggest trap in this field is the repository-level licence. **The pyrfume-data repository is MIT-licensed, yet its own paper says the collection is "intended for non-commercial purposes under FAIR use principles, except where licenses permit commercial use"** ([Pyrfume, Sci Data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11557823/)). Several of its manifests state that "Pyrfume developers make no claims concerning copyright" ([goodscents manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/goodscents/manifest.toml)). The repository has also received no commits since September 2025 ([pyrfume-data](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data)). The same logic applies to Hou 2026: an MIT licence on a repository built mainly from van Gemert's commercial book cannot relicense that book's data ([PMC13558726](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/)).

The sources clearly usable for commercial training, with attribution, are **Keller & Vosshall 2016 (CC BY 4.0), Keller 2012 (CC BY), Ma 2021 (Etalab 2.0), the 2025 lay-panel set (CC BY 4.0), Lee 2023 Data S3 and M2OR (both Apache-2.0), and the DREAM repository** (MIT, derived from the CC BY Keller data). Li 2025's Zenodo deposit (CC BY 4.0) joins them once its upstream PubChem and literature sources are audited. PubChem's HSDB-sourced rows are public domain ([data.gov HSDB](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/hazardous-substances-data-bank-hsdb-28053)), but the Haz-Map rows are "all rights reserved" and must be dropped ([PUG-View](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug_view/annotations/heading/JSON?heading=Odor%20Threshold&heading_type=Compound&page=1)). The EPA 1992 guide is presumptively public domain as a US federal work. Nagata's table qualifies if the Japanese Ministry's Public Data License 1.0, which permits commercial use and is compatible with CC BY 4.0, covers this third-party-authored PDF ([MoE copyright](https://www.env.go.jp/en/utility/copyright.html); [PDL 1.0](https://www.digital.go.jp/en/resources/open_data/public_data_license_v1.0)).

Mayhew 2022 sends conflicting signals. The paper says "there are no restrictions on the availability of these data", and the repository's LICENSE file reads as Apache-2.0. However, GitHub reports the licence as "NOASSERTION", the article itself is CC BY-NC-ND, and 387 of its labels trace back to van Gemert ([PMC9169660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169660/); [OlfactorySpace](https://github.com/emayhew/OlfactorySpace); [pyrfume manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/mayhew_2022/manifest.toml)). The Abraham, Wakayama and Ravia tables in pyrfume are also unclear, because they are copied from journal articles that carry no open licence.

On the other side of the line, several datasets are explicitly non-commercial. The Leffingwell set is "freely available for research use under the CC-BY-NC license" ([pyrfume readme](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/leffingwell/leffingwell_readme.pdf)), and by inheritance so is the OpenPOM GS-LF training set that merges it with Good Scents ([OpenPOM](https://github.com/BioMachineLearning/openpom)). FlavorDB2 is CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 ([FlavorDB2](https://cosylab.iiitd.edu.in/flavordb2/)), FooDB is CC BY-NC 4.0 ([pyrfume manifest](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data/blob/main/foodb/manifest.toml)), and Arctander, SmellSpace and ChemFOnt are all CC BY-NC 4.0 ([ChemFOnt](https://chemfont.ca/simple/license)). Permission or payment is required for Good Scents, bulk use of Leibniz-LSB, VCF, van Gemert, AIHA, Devos, OlfactionBase and Flavornet, and Fragrantica's terms prohibit scraping outright ([Fragrantica ToS](https://www.fragrantica.com/terms-of-service.phtml)).

The legal backdrop depends on jurisdiction (this is not legal advice). In the US, individual threshold numbers are facts. Protection covers only the selection and arrangement of a compilation, plus any contract or terms-of-service obligations. The EU adds a sui generis database right that lasts 15 years and bars extraction of a "substantial part" of a database, including by repeated small extractions ([Database right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database_right)). The EU's text-and-data-mining exceptions split in two. Article 3 protects research organizations, and contracts cannot override it. Article 4 covers commercial mining but yields to machine-readable opt-outs ([Kluwer Copyright Blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/copyright-blog/the-new-copyright-directive-text-and-data-mining-articles-3-and-4/)).

Whether model weights trained on non-commercial data inherit the restriction is unresolved. Conservative practice keeps such checkpoints out of products. Two published projects model workable patterns. Wu et al. release only "numeric targets and citation pointers" plus re-download scripts ([arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426v1)). Fichtelmann releases a scraper instead of the scraped data ([arXiv 2512.08683](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08683)).

## Two to four orders of between-study noise dictate the harmonization recipe

Harmonization matters because raw literature thresholds are extraordinarily noisy. A 2023 methods review found that values in compilations or published before 2003 "rarely have an accuracy of less than three orders of magnitude". Standardized modern methods narrow the spread to one or two orders ([PubMed 37393967](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37393967/)). A 2025 analysis of 63 indoor VOCs found **46,316-fold variation for butanal**. Measurement method shifted values systematically, in the order squeeze bottle > dynamic olfactometry > triangle bag > GC-O. Western panels gave thresholds **2.15 orders of magnitude higher** than Asian panels ([Building & Environment 2025](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025BuEnv.28013122R/abstract)).

Smaller choices move the numbers too. The threshold-calculation method alone put butyric acid at 2,741 µg/L by 3-AFC versus 5,592 µg/L by TST, and panel type shifted 2-phenylethanol from 871 µg/L among experts to 2,042 µg/L among consumers ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)). Recognition thresholds run up to two orders of magnitude above detection thresholds ([Hou 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/)). Stereochemistry matters: the threshold ratio between α-ionone's two enantiomers varied over four orders of magnitude across 63 subjects ([PubMed 2914071](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2914071/)). Impurities matter most of all, because thresholds span so many orders of magnitude that, in Mayhew's words, "even high purity (e.g., 99%) is insufficient" ([PMC9169660](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169660/)). Even Nagata's single-lab repeats varied up to about fivefold ([MoE PDF](https://www.env.go.jp/en/air/odor/measure/02_3_2.pdf)), and Abraham estimated Nagata's self-consistency at 0.66 log units ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/chemse/article/37/3/207/325917)).

Prior work has handled this noise with five aggregation strategies: quality screening plus a geometric mean (EPA 1992, Devos), rescaling other datasets onto a reference scale (Abraham's "Nagata scale"), a log-space median (Li; Wu, who also winsorizes the tails), a modal-bin filter plus geometric mean (Hou), and the lowest reported value (Oliveira 2023). The last biases labels toward sensitive panels and suits safety screening rather than central-tendency modeling ([PMC10589844](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10589844/)).

The recipe below combines the most explicit published pipelines ([Hou 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/); [Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)) with standard cheminformatics tooling ([RDKit MolStandardize](https://www.rdkit.org/docs/source/rdkit.Chem.MolStandardize.rdMolStandardize.html); [PubChem PUG REST](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/docs/pug-rest)).

| Stage | Choice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Record schema | Keep every raw observation: source, year, matrix, endpoint, method, panel size and type, raw value and unit, purity and chirality flags | Aggregates stay recomputable; spread becomes a per-label noise estimate |
| Identifiers | Resolve CAS through PubChem's `name` namespace, falling back to the name; store CID, isomeric SMILES, full InChIKey; flag names that map to several CIDs | A live query resolves `78-93-3` to CID 6569 ([PUG REST](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/rest/pug/compound/name/78-93-3/property/SMILES,ConnectivitySMILES,InChIKey/JSON)) |
| Structures | RDKit `Cleanup`, then `LargestFragmentChooser`, then `Uncharger`; drop records whose round-trip changes heavy-atom topology | Strips salts and solvents without silent structure changes |
| Keys | Aggregate on the full InChIKey; group splits on the 14-character skeleton | Enantiomers differ in potency but must never straddle train and test |
| Units | Air: log10 mol/m³ (mg/m³ = ppm × MW / 24.45 at 25 °C); water: log10 mol/L | Molar basis removes molecular-weight artifacts ([AIHA calculator](https://www.aiha.org/ih-calculator-app/calcs/conversion-concentration.html)) |
| Matrix | Separate heads or a matrix covariate; use Henry's-law constants as features, not to convert labels | Converting water thresholds to air is physically motivated but unvalidated as a label transform |
| Endpoint | Detection only in the primary target; recognition as its own task | Recognition runs up to 100× higher |
| Aggregation | Keep the modal order-of-magnitude bin and its two neighbors (Hou), or apply a log-space MAD filter; then geometric mean; store n, SD and range | Gives per-label uncertainty for weighted or Huber losses |
| Source offsets | Learn a source or method offset, or evaluate within one protocol family | Mirrors Abraham's rescaling onto one scale |
| Censoring | Treat odorless labels as "threshold above tested amount" and M2OR non-responders as EC50 lower bounds | Uses tens of thousands of negatives without inventing values |
| Splits | Report both random and scaffold or similarity-cluster splits; keep all matrices of a molecule in one fold; add a prospective external set | Random splits overstate generalization |
| Loss | Huber (δ ≈ 1.5) with winsorization fitted on the training fold only | Cut threshold MSE from 0.658 to 0.612 in Wu et al. |

Intensity data needs different handling. Condition every rating on log dilution and solvent. Fit per-molecule curves wherever two or more concentrations exist (Keller 2016, Ravia, Wakayama). Never use pyrfume's concentration-averaged targets. For iso-intensity designs, regress the chosen concentration. Receptor data must stay separate from insect data: mixing DoOR's normalized units with M2OR's molar EC50s in one target is not defensible.

The resulting training set is best organized as a multi-head corpus. No single potency label covers enough molecules on its own. The scale figures below are my estimates, because the overlaps between sources have not been computed.

## Honest baselines land near 0.8 log units, not R² 0.94

Published threshold models cluster tightly once you account for how they were split. Across roughly 1,000 compounds in one medium, gradient-boosted trees on standard descriptors or fingerprints reach **R² of about 0.77–0.83 and RMSE of about 0.8–0.9 log units on random splits**. The best-documented result is Hou 2026's XGBoost model. It reached test R² 0.83 and RMSE 0.83, and on 177 thresholds the authors measured themselves, **87% of predictions fell within tenfold and 97.7% within a hundredfold** ([PMC13558726](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/)). That RMSE sits at the label-noise floor set by Abraham's 0.82 log-unit fit and Nagata's 0.66 log-unit self-consistency. Random-split accuracy is therefore already saturated.

Scaffold splits tell a harsher story. Wu et al.'s best graph neural network on a Bemis–Murcko split reached a standardized MSE of about 0.60, which implies roughly 40% of variance explained ([arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)). Li 2025's **R² 0.94 and RMSE 0.44** lies below the noise floor even though a scaffold-aware split is reported ([PMC12654368](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12654368/)). Together with the 716-versus-992 row mismatch, that makes it a result to replicate before trusting.

For intensity, the ceiling depends mostly on how stimuli were delivered. Pellegrino's r = 0.87 compares with a panel test–retest of r = 0.97 under controlled headspace concentration ([PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)). Glass jars give lower reliability than bags: test–retest r of 0.72–0.81 versus 0.86–0.89 ([Andres et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/chemse/bjag022)). Accordingly, DREAM's population-level r = 0.78 on paraffin-oil dilutions sits closer to its own data ceiling than it looks ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28219971/)).

Foundation models have not beaten simple baselines on potency. MoLFormer showed "no clear incremental predictive value" over an RDKit-plus-Morgan baseline on the Keller intensity data ([arXiv 2607.24848](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24848)). ChemBERTa-2 and CheMeleon lost to an MLP on RDKit descriptors for ordinal strength ([arXiv 2512.08683](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08683)). The Principal Odor Map beat an SVM on the Abraham thresholds through a linear probe but reported no number, and its authors state it "cannot predict suprathreshold intensity" ([PMC11898014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11898014/)). Large language models did better on pairwise intensity when given compound names than SMILES strings, which points to lexical association rather than structural reasoning ([arXiv 2604.00002](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.00002v1)).

Physical features dominate every interpretable model: size, polarity, vapor pressure, logP. That argues for joint vapor-pressure and threshold training, or explicit transport features, as the cheapest prior. Three metrics should be standard: fold-error rates (share of predictions within 3× and 10×), scaffold-split RMSE, and comparison against each compound's spread across sources. No shared benchmark reporting all three exists.

## Conclusion

"Odor potency" is not one learnable quantity. It is at least four measurements: the detection threshold, the slope of intensity above threshold, perfumery strength class, and receptor EC50. The free data for each covers a largely different set of molecules. The workable architecture is therefore a shared molecular encoder with separate heads for each medium and measurement type. Vapor pressure and receptor EC50 serve as auxiliary tasks, and censored losses turn tens of thousands of "no response" and "odorless" records into usable constraints. Because the best random-split threshold models already sit at the label-noise floor, further architecture search will buy little. The step changes are in the labels. A commercial team can license VCF or Leibniz-LSB data. Better still, it can measure a few hundred scaffold-diverse compounds under one protocol, such as Nalophan-bag delivery with gLMS ratings and GC-O purity checks, or Czerny-style water thresholds. Enantiomer pairs should be included, because current models ignore stereochemistry.

The clearest open opportunity is a benchmark. No scaffold-split threshold benchmark with per-compound inter-source variance exists. A license-clean seed for one already does: HSDB rows, EPA 1992, Nagata, and the CC BY intensity sets. Whoever turns that seed into a public benchmark with fold-error metrics will set the field's reference point. It would also let reviewers spot claims like R² 0.94 on mixed-source thresholds as likely leakage, not progress.
