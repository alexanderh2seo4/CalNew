# Better labels, not more papers, unlock odor potency

The largest cheap gain in odor-potency data comes from modeling the noise already in the literature, not from adding sources. The same compound's threshold differs by two to three orders of magnitude between studies, yet measured method and panel effects explain only about 0.2–0.5 log units of that spread. A hierarchical consensus model with censored and ordinal labels can be built in weeks at near-zero cost. Literature mining is the next step, and it is technically routine, legal and inexpensive for a researcher at a German university. §60d UrhG permits research text-and-data mining (TDM) of lawfully accessed papers, and contracts cannot override it. DEAL subscriptions plus the Elsevier, Wiley and Springer TDM routes cover most of the paywalled core. A 10,000-paper LLM extraction run costs roughly **$200–450 in tokens**. The real costs are **4–8 person-weeks of engineering and 50–400 hours of human checking**. The yield is mostly provenance-rich repeat measurements and post-2010, matrix-specific values, not thousands of new molecules, because most papers reuse thresholds from a few classic compilations. The best untapped sources are a data agreement with Leibniz-LSB@TUM in Freising (more than 1,700 odorants), fragrance patents (an estimated 300–1,500 thresholds for novel analogues) and "odorless" and ordinal strength labels (thousands of molecules). Grey literature, safety data sheets and Wikidata add almost nothing. New measurements are the most expensive route, at an estimated €300–650 per compound in-house or €100–200k for 300 compounds, but they are the only way to get labels below the literature noise floor. The sequence that follows from this has four steps. First, consensus modeling. Second, partnerships and a mining pilot validated on a gold set. Third, a patent branch. Fourth, a measurement program driven by active learning, started only after learning curves show that label noise, not molecule count, is what limits the model.

## Ten thousand papers mention potency, but most recycle the same thresholds

The literature is larger than the curated datasets suggest, but it is not a large reservoir of *independent* measurements. Live OpenAlex queries for the core terms ("odor threshold", "odor activity value", "aroma extract dilution analysis", "flavor dilution factor", "olfactometry" and variants) return **9,945 works in titles or abstracts** and about **27,000 when full text is searched**. Of the title/abstract set, 41% is open access ([OpenAlex API](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=title_and_abstract.search:%22odor%20threshold%22%7C%22odor%20activity%20value%22&group_by=open_access.oa_status)). The field is growing fast. The union counted 360 papers in 2019 and 843 in 2025, and 2026 had already reached 871 by September. More than half the corpus is therefore newer than 2018 ([OpenAlex API](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=title_and_abstract.search:%22odor%20threshold%22&group_by=primary_location.source.id)).

That growth matters because the largest classic compilation stops at 2010. The van Gemert 2011 edition gathers **about 22,000 threshold values from about 2,930 references up to 2010**. That covers roughly 1,790 compounds in air, 1,860 in water and 760 in other media ([Leffingwell & Associates](http://www.leffingwell.com/bacis2.htm)). A literature pass can add the post-2010 papers that no compilation holds. The largest recent open compilation, Yang et al. 2026, found **848 compounds across 11 matrices in 146 studies since 1952** ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)). Hou et al. put the whole population of experimentally measured compounds at "only around 1800" ([Hou et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/)). Mining will not break that ceiling by much.

The reason is how the papers use thresholds. Most odor-activity-value (OAV) papers divide their own measured concentrations by a threshold *cited* from van Gemert, Rychlik and colleagues, or the Grosch and Schieberle group. So the typical mined OAV row is a citation copy, not a new measurement. Papers that measure thresholds themselves are a much smaller group. They come mainly from the Freising flavor-chemistry groups, Chemical Senses psychophysics, and structure–odor studies of synthetic odorants. FD factors and OAVs depend on the matrix and the study. They are useful "potency in context" labels, not intrinsic molecular properties.

No published study has used LLMs or text mining to extract thresholds, OAVs or FD factors at scale. The 716-compound Li et al. set was assembled from existing databases and aggregated by median ([PMC12654368](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12654368/)). The scientific value of mining is therefore specific. It yields **observation-level records that keep medium, method, panel size, detection-versus-recognition, year and cited source**. Those are the variables that median-aggregated tables discard. It also yields the citation chains needed to collapse copies back to their original measurement.

The corpus also contains noise that has to be filtered out. "Olfactometry" pulls in environmental odour-emission work from Water Science & Technology and Chemical Engineering Transactions. "Odor intensity" pulls in clinical smell testing. A topic or journal filter reduces the realistic target to an estimated **8,000–12,000 candidate papers**. Perhaps 5,000–8,000 of them contain quantitative potency tables, a rough figure that should be checked on a 200-paper random sample.

## German law and DEAL contracts make research mining legal and nearly free

For non-commercial research, the legal position in Germany is unusually favorable. **§60d UrhG** implements Article 3 of the EU DSM Directive. It allows research organisations and individual non-commercial researchers to reproduce lawfully accessible works for TDM, to share the copies within a defined research group, and to keep them securely for as long as the research needs them ([§60d UrhG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/urhg/__60d.html)). **§60g(1)** makes contract terms that restrict these uses unenforceable ([§60g UrhG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/urhg/__60g.html)). **§87c** extends the same research-TDM permission to the sui generis database right ([§87c UrhG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/urhg/__87c.html)).

The courts have so far read these provisions broadly. In Kneschke v LAION, the Hamburg district court held in 2024 that copying to build an AI training dataset fell under §60d ([EUIPO summary](https://www.euipo.europa.eu/en/law/recent-case-law/germany-hamburg-district-court-310-o-22723-laion-v-robert-kneschke)). The Hamburg Higher Regional Court dismissed the appeal in December 2025. It confirmed a broad reading of "scientific research" and held that a natural-language opt-out in website terms is not machine-readable. A further appeal to the Federal Court of Justice was allowed, and its status was not checked ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/blogs/mse-today/2025/robert-kneschke-v-laion)).

The general exception in **§44b** is the one that covers commercial mining. It yields to machine-readable reservations, and it requires the mining copies to be deleted once they are no longer needed ([§44b UrhG](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/urhg/__44b.html)).

The key legal distinction is between facts and compilations. Numeric threshold values are facts, so a table of compound, structure, value, unit, medium, method and DOI does not reproduce the papers. Systematically re-extracting a protected compilation is different. Van Gemert, VCF and the Leibniz-LSB@TUM database are exactly the targets of the database right. Research TDM permits the mining copy, but it does not clearly permit *publishing* a re-assembled substantial part of such a database. The robust path is to mine the primary papers and trace each value cited from van Gemert back to its original source, which is also better science.

Two lines should not be crossed. Papers from Sci-Hub, LibGen or Anna's Archive are not "lawfully accessible", so no TDM exception covers them. Commercial use changes everything. §60d does not apply to a project under the controlling influence of a company with preferential access to results. It then falls back to §44b, which means honoring opt-outs and paying for publisher licences. Whether a model trained under §60d may later be commercialized is unresolved, so LMU legal or tech-transfer advice is needed before any spin-off.

Access routes differ by publisher, and one publisher is the bottleneck.

J Agric Food Chem is the single most important journal. It is about 93% closed, and ACS handles TDM "on a project by project basis", so the request should go in first because it runs through people, not an API.

UB LMU's general e-media terms prohibit robot downloading and direct researchers to contact the library about TDM ([UB LMU](https://www.ub.uni-muenchen.de/ausleihe-online/e-medien-login/index.html)). §60g overrides that contract term. However, §60d(6) still lets publishers throttle or block traffic, so the cooperative API route is far more reliable than asserting the exception against a blocking platform.

The precedent is encouraging. The Ceder group mined **4.2 million papers** from six publishers after a university librarian arranged explicit TDM agreements. They processed only post-2000 HTML/XML and published only the extracted facts with DOIs ([Kononova et al. 2019](https://ceder.berkeley.edu/publications/2019_olga_text-mining_dataset.pdf)). An odor corpus is 100–400 times smaller.

## A 50,000-paper pipeline costs under $3,000 in tokens; people are the real expense

The extraction technology is mature enough that tokens are no longer the constraint. Publisher XML is the best input, because tables arrive already structured, footnotes included. For PDFs, **MinerU 2.5 posts the best open-source table score on OmniDocBench (TEDS 88.2)** ([MinerU2.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.22186)). **Marker 2** claims about five times MinerU's throughput, though that comparison comes from Marker's own developer ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/07/24/datalab-marker-v2-vs-mineru-docling-and-liteparse-benchmark-breakdown/)).

No benchmark tests what makes threshold tables hard: units that appear only in a header row or a footnote, superscript letters that link a value to a method or a reference, ranges and inequalities such as "<0.01", and 1980s scan-quality PDFs. A TEDS of 0.88 can hide a mis-attached superscript that changes a value's meaning. The practical fix is to give the extraction model both the parsed table and a rendered image of that table page.

Chemical identity is a solved problem when handled as a cascade. Take the CAS number if the paper prints one, otherwise look the name up in PubChem, then try CIR, which resolves trivial flavor names such as "furaneol" or "sotolon" ([CACTUS](https://cactus.nci.nih.gov/blog/?tag=iupac-name)). Systematic IUPAC names go to OPSIN, which reports **≥99.8% precision and 98.7–99.2% recall** ([OPSIN, JCIM 2011](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/ci100384d)), and every resolution is checked for InChIKey agreement between two resolvers. Stereochemistry needs its own field. PubChem often maps a trivial name to the racemate, so enantiomer-specific thresholds must never be merged into a stereo-unspecified record.

Extraction accuracy is good but not good enough to trust unchecked. **ChatExtract with GPT-4 reached 90.8% precision and 87.7% recall** on bulk modulus by asking follow-up "are you sure" questions ([Polak & Morgan, Nat Commun 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-45914-8)). The polymer project that mined **681,000 articles into more than a million records** saw F1 fall to 0.67 for glass-transition temperature, a messily reported property ([Gupta et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43246-024-00708-9)). A 2026 study converged on expert-defined evidence standards, cross-checks between models and human resolution of disputed cases, noting that autonomous agents "either missed or hallucinated references" ([arXiv 2608.19025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19025)).

The errors to expect for odor data are specific. Values get paired with the wrong row or shifted between air and water columns. Units get confused across µg/L, ng/L, µg/kg and ppb (v/v or w/w). The medium is often stated only in the Methods section. Detection and recognition thresholds get conflated, cited values get mistaken for new measurements, and models invent values for compounds named in the text but absent from the table.

The cheapest safeguard is a grounding check: every extracted number must appear verbatim in the source text. After that come unit normalization, a plausibility window, and a requirement that FD factors be powers of two or three. The costly layers are dual-model agreement and outlier flags for values more than two log units from the compound's median.

Token costs, at September 2026 list prices with the 50% batch discount, assume 14,000 input and 1,500 output tokens per paper ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [BenchLM Claude pricing](https://benchlm.ai/anthropic/api-pricing)):

| Model (batch) | 10k papers | 50k papers |
|---|---|---|
| Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite | ~$29 | ~$144 |
| Claude Haiku 4.5 | ~$108 | ~$538 |
| Claude Sonnet 5 | ~$215 | ~$1,075 |
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | ~$230 | ~$1,150 |
| Claude Opus 5 | ~$538 | ~$2,688 |
| Sonnet 5 + Gemini 3.1 Pro dual check | ~$445 | ~$2,225 |

A pre-filter changes these figures further. A regex over table captions, followed by a Flash-Lite classifier costing about $0.0003 per paper, can pass only 20–40% of papers to the expensive model. That cuts Sonnet-class spend on 50,000 papers to about $215–430. Parsing 500,000 pages takes about **66 A100-hours with MinerU or 19 B200-hours with Marker**, roughly $100–200 of rented GPU time.

These are estimates, and three of their inputs are assumptions: token counts per paper, GPU rental rates, and a pricing landscape that changes monthly. The conclusion holds regardless: **tokens cost less than one week of a research assistant**.

The human side dominates the budget. Every figure below is an estimate:

| Item | Effort |
|---|---|
| Gold set: 100–200 fully annotated papers, needed to measure per-field precision and recall before scaling | 35–100 hours |
| Adjudicating dual-model disagreements, grouped by table | 50–150 hours at 50k papers |
| 5% stratified audit | 40–170 hours |
| Engineering: journal pipeline | 4–8 person-weeks |
| Engineering: patent branch | +2–4 person-weeks |

A focused 10,000-paper run needs about a third of the human effort. Expected residual error after validation is about 2–5% of records, extrapolated from roughly 90% raw precision. It must be measured on the gold set, not assumed.

## Partnerships and patents beat grey literature among untapped sources

The most valuable source is 20 km from Munich. The **Leibniz-LSB@TUM Odorant Database holds more than 1,700 odorants extracted from more than 700 publications**, with thresholds in several matrices. Its terms encourage scientific use with attribution but forbid systematic downloading or reproduction without permission ([Leibniz-LSB copyright](https://www.leibniz-lsb.de/en/databases/leibniz-lsbtum-odorant-database/copyright-and-citation)). The group also maintains an odorant–receptor database ([Leibniz-LSB Receptor DB](https://www.leibniz-lsb.de/en/databases/leibniz-lsbtum-receptor-database/)).

This database already distils much of the Freising flavor-chemistry output that sits in J Agric Food Chem. A data agreement with the group therefore substitutes for most of the hardest ACS mining. The strongest proposal pairs a data-use agreement for the threshold table, with co-authorship and a no-redistribution clause if the group needs one, with a joint re-measurement of 100–200 anchor compounds under one protocol that estimates the inter-study offsets inside their own database. That second element gives the curators something they cannot easily get alone.

Two published compilations are next. Yang et al. 2026 is Wiley open access and adds method annotations to 848 compounds. Those annotations are exactly the covariates a random-effects model needs. Whether the per-study table is in the supplement is unverified ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)). Sun, Jia and Xi trained on **1,741 air thresholds spanning 10⁻⁹ to 10⁸ mg/m³**, apparently the largest structure-resolved air set, and a request to the authors costs nothing ([SSRN 7070562](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7070562)).

Fragrance patents are the best source of *new chemistry*. Givaudan-style patents describe GC-olfactometry protocols in which panelists sniff back-calculated concentrations and the panel average becomes the threshold ([WO2006133592A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2006133592A1/en)). They report ultra-potent structures such as a vetiver odorant at **29 pg/L air** ([PubMed 33315304](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33315304/)). Procter & Gamble product patents sort perfume raw materials into threshold classes with numeric cut-offs such as ≤50 ppb. Those are clean interval labels ([US5919752A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5919752A/en)).

SureChEMBL already links patent text to structures ([SureChEMBL, NAR](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4702887/)). Its BigQuery mirror, however, was last updated in 2018 ([patents-public-data #25](https://github.com/google/patents-public-data/issues/25)). The yield estimate is **300–1,500 values, mostly for novel musk, amber, woody and marine analogues**. Close structural neighbours like these carry structure–activity information that the food literature lacks. The patent branch would cost 2–4 extra engineering weeks and about $130 in tokens for 10,000 patents after section routing. The data has two biases: patents report favourable examples, and each company uses one in-house method. That second bias suits a lab random effect.

Old compilations add replicates rather than molecules. **ASTM DS 48A (1978)** lists medium, purity, value, units and literature source for each entry and stays under US copyright until about 2073 ([HathiTrust](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/000176905)). The EU database right on it has expired, so individual values can be re-extracted into a new structure, though scans and verbatim tables cannot be published. **Devos et al. 1990**, "the first homogeneous inventory of weighted and averaged" thresholds for over 500 chemicals, can be borrowed through Internet Archive and is the natural calibration anchor ([OUP](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/standardized-human-olfactory-thresholds-9780199631469); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/standardizedhuma0000unse)). The **USDA papers** by Buttery and Guadagni are US government works and therefore public domain ([17 U.S.C. §105](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)); they pair water thresholds with air–water partition coefficients measured in the same lab. Converting all three takes several person-weeks of OCR and name resolution, since DS 48A identifies compounds by Wiswesser notation only. Their payoff is the multiple independent measurements per compound, which variance estimation needs.

Buying licences is cheap but rarely buys ML rights. VCF online costs **€1,485 a year** ([VCF](https://www.vcf-online.nl/)), and the offline BACIS VCF 2000 costs **US$2,775 per PC** ([Leffingwell](http://www.leffingwell.com/bacis1.htm)). Both are licensed for consultation. Neither grants extraction or redistribution rights, so any purchase needs an explicit written ML or TDM clause. The van Gemert order domain now shows a parked page ([thresholdcompilation.com](http://www.thresholdcompilation.com/)). Its content is also upstream of most free datasets already, so a licence would add an estimated few hundred new compounds at most.

## Hierarchical consensus turns three orders of noise into usable labels

The single highest-return step uses data already in hand. The evidence points to a model of the form:

log ODT_observed = compound potency + study/lab offset + method, panel and medium effects + Student-t error,

with censored and interval terms for "<x", ">x" and ranges.

The effects are measurable. On the same 3-AFC data, curve fitting gave 861 µg/L for ethyl hexanoate and the best-estimate-threshold rule gave 507 µg/L. Expert and consumer panels put 2-phenylethanol at 871 and 2,042 µg/L. Both effects are about **0.2–0.5 log10 units**, against reported inter-laboratory spreads of two to three orders of magnitude ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)). Most of the dispersion is therefore study-specific error: impurities, delivery, unit mistakes and old static methods. A robust likelihood with lab random effects is built to absorb that kind of error. Simple median pooling is not.

The approach already has a working precedent. **Abraham et al. 2012 rescaled three other labs onto Nagata's scale and grew the dataset from 193 to 353 compounds with no loss of fit** (SD 0.830 before, 0.819 after) ([Abraham et al. 2012](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9b72q2v9)). Devos 1990 applied an earlier, fixed-effects version of the same idea.

Drug discovery shows what naive pooling costs. Landrum and Riniker found that combining IC50 values from different assays is "a source of significant noise", and that matching assay metadata reduces it ([JCIM 2024](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jcim.4c00049)). No published hierarchical Bayesian meta-analysis of odor thresholds with lab random effects was found. This is an open niche. It would yield a consensus value with a posterior uncertainty for every compound, usable as a training target or sample weight, together with estimated lab biases and automatic outlier flags.

Censored and ordinal labels add thousands of molecules at almost no cost. Svensson and colleagues showed across 15 AstraZeneca assays that **Tobit likelihoods** trained on censored labels improve likelihood and calibration, with the largest gains above **35% censoring** ([arXiv 2409.04313](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04313)). "Odorless" labels become compound-specific censoring bounds, because the threshold must exceed the saturated vapor concentration that vapor pressure supplies. The 1,924 Mayhew labels feed this head directly, with one caution: **22% of their "odorous" molecules turned out to be odorless compounds contaminated by impurities** ([Mayhew et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169660/)). Ordinal strength classes fit a cumulative-link head. The published four-class set now covers **2,393 molecules**, Good Scents and PubChem labels agree at a weighted κ of 0.83, and the best ensemble reaches 72% accuracy; the PubChem part is openly released, while the Good Scents part must be re-scraped ([Fichtelmann & Westermayr 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)). Within-study ranking losses complete the toolkit, cancelling lab offsets without an explicit random-effects model, since rankings inside one study are more reliable than absolute values.

Physics supplies the prior. Vapor pressure, water solubility and log Kow alone explain **r² = 0.769** of log-threshold variance ([Rodríguez et al. 2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ffj.2076)). Training the ML model on the residual from a transport-property baseline is typically more sample-efficient at around 1,500 labels.

Air-to-water conversion through Henry's law is physically motivated but unvalidated at scale. Ethanol–water matrices behave non-monotonically, and orthonasal and retronasal thresholds preserve rank (Spearman 0.932) but not absolute values ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)). Conversion should therefore enter the model as a medium offset with its own uncertainty. The compounds that already have both direct air and water thresholds provide an immediate, free check of the conversion slope.

For intensity, the Hill equation beat Stevens, Beidler and Fechner on 62 odorants at seven or more concentrations. A neural network then predicted intensity at r = 0.87 against a test–retest reliability of 0.97 ([Pellegrino et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)). Fitted curve parameters, not averaged ratings, are the right intensity target.

One honest caveat applies throughout. No odor-specific study quantifies the gains from any of these methods. The only way to know is a retrospective benchmark on the pooled data that reports the noise ceiling. That ceiling is the spread across sources for compounds measured more than once, and published models near 0.8 log units RMSE may already sit on it.

## Measuring 300 new compounds costs €100–200k and a year of panel time

Generating data is the only route to labels cleaner than the literature, and the method is standard. **ASTM E679-19** prescribes a three-alternative forced-choice test up an ascending geometric concentration series. Each panellist's best-estimate threshold is combined into a geometric mean ([ASTM E679-19](https://www.astm.org/Standards/E679.htm)). A recent review puts the minimum at **15 assessors** for a valid result ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)). EN 13725 screens assessors on n-butanol: the geometric mean of their individual estimates must fall between 20 and 80 ppb ([PMC3231359](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3231359/)).

No published per-compound cost exists for an academic threshold lab, so the figures here are estimates. With 16–20 panellists and six to eight dilution steps, one compound in one matrix costs **about €300–650**: roughly €150 of panel pay, €100–220 of technician time, €20–60 of consumables and €50–200 for a GC-MS purity check. Fatigue limits throughput to **about 3–8 compounds a week, or 150–300 a year**. Sniffing-bottle testing needs under €5k of equipment, while a commercial dynamic olfactometer such as the Olfasense TO8/TO9 or Scentroid SS600 probably costs €40k–150k plus an odour-neutral room; no public prices were found, so quotes are needed ([Olfasense TO8](https://www.olfasense.com/odour-measurement-equipment/instruments/to8-evolution-olfactometer/)). Buying 500 diverse odorants would run an estimated €25k–75k, with thiols, methoxypyrazines and single enantiomers pushing the upper tail.

The dominant hidden cost is purity. A 0.1% impurity that is 10,000 times more potent than the target determines the measured threshold. GC-O of each sample is therefore not optional.

Outsourcing is possible but awkward. Germany has many accredited EN 13725 labs: TÜV SÜD, DEKRA, ODOCON, BUB, Olfasense and others ([TÜV SÜD](https://www.tuvsud.com/de-de/branchen/produzierende-industrie/anlagenbau-betrieb/umwelt-klimaschutz/geruchsgutachten-olfaktometrie); [ODOCON](https://www.odocon.de/umweltanalytik/olfaktometrie)). None publishes prices. These labs measure emission bags, not pure compounds, and their panels of about four assessors are thinner than threshold work needs. The workable model is the "secondary reference odour mass" approach: supply a gravimetrically prepared bag of a pure odorant and divide its concentration by the measured odour concentration ([Environ Sci Eur 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12302-025-01179-6)). The estimate is **€500–1,200 per compound** for two to three replicate bags. That gives a consistent single-protocol source, not a population estimate.

Regulation at LMU is manageable. Every LMU member doing research on humans must apply through the ethikPool-LMU portal, mandatory since April 2024 ([LMU Ethikkommission](https://www.med.lmu.de/de/fakultaet/wer-wir-sind/gremien-kommissionen/ethikkommission/)). A non-drug, non-device study needs a protocol, German-language participant consent, data-protection consent and institutional sign-off ([LMU Nicht AMG/MPDG](https://www.med.lmu.de/de/fakultaet/wer-wir-sind/gremien-kommissionen/ethikkommission/nicht-amg-mpdg/)). Published fees cover only drug and device trials (€3,500), so a healthy-volunteer smell study is probably fee-free. That is unverified. Plan one to three months for the vote. Consent must explicitly cover open publication of pseudonymised panel data.

Cheaper proxies help, but they cannot replace thresholds. For musks, receptor EC50 tracked human threshold at r = 0.87 ([Current Biology 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982223003044)), but across most chemistry the relevant receptor is unknown, and a missing receptor produces a false "weak"; receptor data belongs in auxiliary tasks, not labels. At-home crowdsourcing cannot control headspace, and the largest lay-panel sets measure intensity at fixed doses rather than thresholds ([Sci Data 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-025-04644-2)), so crowd work suits "which is stronger" comparisons. GC-O dilution to threshold measures many compounds per run and can enter the hierarchical model as its own noisy method.

The design matters more than the count. In a structurally analogous log-scale property, one round of active learning cut RMSE by up to **0.3 log units** ([PMC12224155](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12224155/)). The recommended portfolio splits the budget roughly equally across anchor compounds spanning six or more log units, measured in every session to calibrate lab offsets; literature compounds whose published values spread more than 1.5 log units; and scaffolds where the model is uncertain, chosen by ensemble disagreement weighted for diversity. At about five compounds a week, **300 compounds take 12–18 months and cost an estimated €100k–200k all-in, including staff**. The contract-lab route runs €150k–350k.

Four things are worth skipping. SDS mining, Wikidata and fan sites return nothing usable. Shadow libraries would taint the dataset and void the §60d defence.

## Conclusion

The literature is not an untapped mine of new molecules. It is an untapped record of *why* measurements disagree. Existing datasets reduce each compound to one median and throw that record away, and that is why published models stall near 0.8 log units. A dataset that keeps every observation with its method, medium, panel and citation lineage can be mined legally and cheaply by a German research group. Paired with a hierarchical model, it would be the first to separate true potency from lab effects. That model-plus-benchmark is both the most publishable output and the precondition for judging whether any new source adds anything.

The commercial question is the main decision to make first. Under §60d, mining, TDM-API access and a Leibniz-LSB collaboration cost almost nothing. A commercial aim removes §60d, turns publisher access into paid licences, and makes self-generated measurements the only fully owned labels. In that case, a few hundred well-chosen, purity-checked thresholds under one protocol are worth more than any volume of mined data.
