# Build your olfactophores — no database holds them

**No open database of olfactophore models exists, and no closed one is distributed either.** Roughly 29 published olfactophore or SOR-geometry models cover the families of interest — muguet, marine/Calone, musk, ambergris, sandalwood, green/galbanum, rose — but every one of them was published as a figure in a fragrance-chemistry journal, most of them paywalled Wiley titles, and not one has ever been released as a machine-readable pharmacophore file (.phar, .ph4, .pml, Phase .phypo, Catalyst .chm). A GitHub search for the term "olfactophore" returns **zero repositories**. The odor databases that do exist — M2OR, OlfactionBase, Pyrfume, OpenPOM, ORDB/HORDE — are 2D molecule↔descriptor or molecule↔receptor-activity resources with no 3D feature geometry, and the general pharmacophore repositories (PharmMapper/PharmTargetDB, Pharmit) are built from druggable targets that exclude olfactory receptors entirely. The closest thing on the open internet is a command-line tool, `bin/olfactophore` inside the GPL-3.0 AROMA repository, which *generates* models on demand rather than storing them. That means anyone wanting a muguet or Calone olfactophore today must rebuild it — and the good news is that the full toolchain to do so (RDKit, CDPKit/CONFORGE, Align-it, Pharmit) is BSD/LGPL/GPL, runs on a MacBook, and costs nothing. The compute story has a sharp shape: fitting a novel molecule to an olfactophore costs **0.1–0.5 seconds, of which >99.9% is conformer generation and ~0.2 ms is the actual pharmacophore match**; the cliff arrives one tier later, at docking against ~400 receptor models (~2.5 CPU-hours per odorant), which the only published benchmark suggests barely works.

## Twenty-nine published models, zero downloadable files

The olfactophore literature is essentially one lineage plus a scattering of one-offs. Philip Kraft's group — at Givaudan through roughly 2020, then Symrise — produced the only continuously active quantitative programme, building HypoGen-style models in Catalyst and later BIOVIA Discovery Studio, trained on measured odor thresholds rather than binary actives. The two *Angewandte* reviews from that lineage, Kraft, Bajgrowicz, Denis and Fráter's "Odds and Trends" (Angew Chem Int Ed 2000, 39, 2980; [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works?search=olfactophore&per-page=100&select=title,publication_year,doi,authorships,primary_location,open_access)) and its twenty-year sequel by Armanino and colleagues ([Angew Chem Int Ed 2020, 59, 16310](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202005719)), are the nearest thing to a consolidated catalogue: both are organised family by family — fruity, marine, green, floral, spicy, woody, amber, musky — with olfactophore figures. Both are closed access. The book *Scent and Chemistry* (2nd ed., Wiley-VCH 2022) devotes §3.10 explicitly to "QSAR: Homology Versus Olfactophore Models" ([TOC](https://content.e-bookshelf.de/media/reading/L-18511499-85192109e6.pdf)).

What the accessible literature actually prints, per family, is thin but not empty:

One finding cuts across two of these families and constrains any reconstruction. Moving the alkyl group on a benzodioxepinone from C7 to C6 flips the odor from marine to lily of the valley: the 6-cyclopropyl analogue has a threshold of **0.065 ng/L air**, in Lilial's range, and the 6-(cyclopropylmethyl) derivative superimposes on Bourgeonal ([Jordi & Kraft, Helv Chim Acta 2018](https://doi.org/10.1002/hlca.201800048)). The marine and muguet olfactophores therefore plausibly share an HBA-plus-aromatic-plus-hydrophobe core and differ mainly in *where* the hydrophobe sits relative to the osmophore — which also explains why new benzodioxepines and benzodioxoles "required a revision of the existing olfactophore models" ([Armanino et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202005719)). Anyone building the two models independently should expect them to overlap and should treat the separation between them as the thing to get right.

The pattern is unmistakable. The rose nitrile paper is the outlier that proves the rule: because CHIMIA is diamond open access, it prints the training set, the activities, the fit statistics and the software versions — everything needed to *re-derive* the model, though still no coordinates. Everything else gives you a picture. The most likely reason is commercial: these models underpin captive-odorant design and patent positions at Givaudan, Firmenich and Symrise, and a machine-readable hypothesis is the asset itself. One useful caution comes from inside the field — Triller, Sell and colleagues showed that OR1D2 activation did not correlate simply with odor percept, and argued that olfactophores are **design tools, not models of perception** ([Chem Biodivers 2008, 5, 862](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1002/cbdv.200890101)).

## The closest alternatives split three ways: ligands, receptors, and one GPL tool

Since no model store exists, the realistic substitutes are the ingredients from which models are made. The **ligand-side** substitute is the OpenPOM curated GoodScents–Leffingwell set, which was downloaded and counted directly: **4,983 molecules × 138 binary odor labels** ([BioMachineLearning/openpom](https://github.com/BioMachineLearning/openpom)). Per-family counts are workable — **muguet 56, lily 71, musk 98, sandalwood 36, amber 101, ozone 39, melon 132, aldehydic 144** — against classical published olfactophores that used only 10–30 actives. But the vocabulary has **no `marine`, `watery` or `ambergris` label at all**, which makes the Calone family the hardest of the requested set: `ozone` (39) is the nearest proxy and `melon` (132) catches the watermelon facet, neither of which is the marine note. The workaround is the raw GoodScents and Leffingwell descriptor strings distributed through Pyrfume, which archives **>20,000 identified odorants across >40 datasets** and retains "marine"/"aquatic" as free text ([Pyrfume, *Scientific Data* 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-024-04051-z); [pyrfume-data](https://github.com/pyrfume/pyrfume-data)). Note also that perfumery "amber" and "ambergris" are different things, so the 101 amber molecules over-count the ambergris family.

The **receptor-side** substitute has changed materially since 2023. M2OR, the reference OR–odorant activity database, holds **51,395 distinct OR–molecule pairs from 75,050 bioassay experiments** across 1,246 receptor sequences — receptor responses, EC50s, assay metadata, and explicitly **no 3D structures, binding sites or pharmacophores** ([M2OR, NAR 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10767820/)). *Licence conflict, stated as found:* the **GitHub snapshot `M2OR_20230428.csv` is Apache-2.0** ([github.com/chemosim-lab/M2OR](https://github.com/chemosim-lab/M2OR)), while the **paper and web-portal data are given as CC-BY 4.0**; both attributions come from their respective sources and a user should check which artifact they are actually redistributing. Experimental OR structures have grown from one in 2023 to roughly a dozen mammalian entries by September 2026 (direct RCSB query), but almost all are engineered consensus receptors or class I fatty-acid receptors. The exception matters enormously here: **PDB 9W45, released 4 March 2026, is mouse Olfr412 — the ortholog of human OR1D2, the bourgeonal/lily-of-the-valley receptor — bound to methyl-*trans*-cinnamate**, with the ligand's benzene ring packing against F207/L255/L199/L260 and the ester oxygens contacting Y259 and Y277 ([Science Advances 2026, PMC13025043](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13025043/)). That is an aromatic + hydrophobic + H-bond-acceptor arrangement — a structure-based muguet olfactophore in all but name, and it did not exist before March 2026. For everything else, GPCRdb's 2025 release added **all ~400 human odorant receptors** as state-specific AlphaFold/RoseTTAFold models ([NAR 2025, 53:D425](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/53/D1/D425/7903362)), with the standing caveat that ECL2 — which forms the lid of the OR binding cavity — "could not be properly modelled" and carries medium-to-low pLDDT ([Nicoli et al., Chem Senses 2025](https://academic.oup.com/chemse/article/doi/10.1093/chemse/bjaf038/8306567)). Musk has named receptors, **OR5AN1 and OR1A1**, the former responding at nanomolar concentrations to musk ketone ([Block et al., PNAS 2018](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1713026115)). **Marine/Calone, ambergris and sandalwood have no deorphanized human receptor in the published literature** — for those three, ligand-based modelling is the only route.

The third substitute is the one artifact that actually does the job: **AROMA/primaryodors** (GPL-3.0) ships `bin/olfactophore`, "a tool for superimposing odorant molecules in space in order to identify the spatial and electrostatic features of collections of odorants, such as those with a common aroma note or the agonists of a given receptor", alongside ~400 OR homology models in **both active and inactive states**, an SDF odorant database with perceptual notes, and a docking pipeline ([github.com/primaryodors/aroma](https://github.com/primaryodors/aroma)). It is a generator, not a library — which is exactly what the absence of a database forces on you.

## Rebuilding a muguet model is an afternoon of BSD-licensed work

The open pharmacophore toolchain is complete and mature, and nothing in it is odor-aware — which turns out not to matter, because every published olfactophore uses standard medicinal-chemistry feature types (HBA, HBD, hydrophobic, aromatic, ionizable) anyway. **RDKit** (BSD-3) supplies feature definitions, 2D pharmacophore fingerprints and `Chem.Pharm3D` alignment and constrained embedding ([RDKit Pharm3D docs](https://www.rdkit.org/docs/source/rdkit.Chem.Pharm3D.Pharmacophore.html)). **CDPKit/CDPL** (LGPL 2.1+) adds both molecule and pharmacophore generation, fingerprints, alignment and screening ([cdpkit.org](https://cdpkit.org/introduction.html)). **Align-it**, the former Silicos-it "Pharao", models features as Gaussian 3D volumes rather than points and is the canonical way to superimpose a set of actives and read off the common arrangement — the original repo is gone but GPL/LGPL forks are maintained ([OliverBScott/align-it](https://github.com/OliverBScott/align-it); [Pharao paper](https://www.silicos-it.be/assets/papers/hdw-pharao-paper.pdf)). **Pharmit** (dual BSD/GPL) handles structure-based search at library scale ([pharmit.csb.pitt.edu](https://pharmit.csb.pitt.edu/)), and **OpenPharmacophore** (MIT), **OpenPharmaco** (MIT) and **PharmacoNet** derive receptor-based models from a complex ([uibcdf/OpenPharmacophore](https://github.com/uibcdf/OpenPharmacophore); [SeonghwanSeo/OpenPharmaco](https://github.com/SeonghwanSeo/OpenPharmaco)).

The stakes justify the effort: lily-of-the-valley odorants account for "over 20% of the total perfumery raw materials market" ([Perfumer & Flavorist](https://img.perfumerflavorist.com/files/base/allured/all/document/2016/04/pf.8041.pdf)), and the 2022 Lilial ban forced the industry into a replacement scramble that produced "a plethora of new 'muguets'" ([Armanino et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.202005719)). The concrete recipe, for muguet: take the 56 `muguet` molecules (or ~127 pooled with `lily`, at the cost of label noise), generate conformers with CDPKit's CONFORGE, superimpose with Align-it or CDPKit, extract the common HBA/aromatic/hydrophobic arrangement, encode it as an RDKit `Pharm3D.Pharmacophore` with distance bounds, and screen with CDPKit or Pharmit. For the first time, that ligand-based model can be **cross-checked against a structure-based one**: run OpenPharmaco or PharmacoNet on PDB 9W45 and compare the receptor-derived feature map to the ligand-derived one. For marine/Calone, the same recipe applies but the actives must be hand-curated from raw GoodScents/Leffingwell descriptor text, and there is no receptor to cross-check against. Two published models are specified well enough to *reconstruct* rather than re-derive: the rose nitrile model (full training table plus fit statistics) and the musk model (three inter-feature distances plus excluded-volume count) — reconstructions of those would be new, citable work in their own right, and would be the first machine-readable olfactophores in existence.

## Conformer generation, not matching, is 99.9% of the per-molecule cost

The single most important compute fact is counter-intuitive: **pharmacophore matching is free, and the 3D ensemble behind it is the entire cost**. Pharmer's worst-case benchmark searched **1,892,172 conformations in 11.3 s of compute** on a 2009-era Core i7-975 — my arithmetic makes that **~167,000 conformers per second**, against MOE's 119.2 s for the same query ([Pharmer, JCIM 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3124593/)). A novel odorant needs ~34 conformers, so the match itself takes **~0.2 ms**. Generating those conformers takes 0.09–6 seconds depending entirely on the generator.

That makes conformer choice the largest practical lever in the whole funnel. On the Platinum Diverse benchmark (2,859 molecules, max 50 conformers, Xeon E5-2630 v3), **CONFORGE averaged 0.09 s per molecule against RDKit ETKDGv3's 5.54–6.22 s — a 61× gap — while reproducing more experimental structures below 0.5 Å RMSD (51.6% vs RDKit's mean RMSD of 0.71–0.74 Å)** ([CONFORGE, JCIM 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10498443/)). The whole 2,859-molecule set took CONFORGE **5 min 21 s** versus Conformator's 2 h 50 min. CONFORGE ships inside CDPKit as the `confgen` CLI ([CDPKit docs](https://cdpkit.org/applications/confgen.html)), so the fast path is also the open path. Scaling that to an Apple-Silicon laptop requires an assumption I state plainly — **assumed, not sourced: a 2026 M-series P-core is ~3× the single-thread throughput of the Haswell Xeon, with 12 usable cores; treat every laptop figure as ±2×**. On that basis CONFORGE runs ~400 mol/s and ETKDGv3 ~6.5 mol/s, so **1,000 molecules take ~2.5 s versus ~2.6 min, 100,000 take ~4 min versus ~4.3 h, and 10 million take ~7 h versus ~18 days** — the last of which is where a cluster becomes mandatory for the slow path and unnecessary for the fast one. Macrocycles break this: musk macrocycles need **342 conformers** rather than ~34, and median generation times rise to 25 s (iCon) to 427 s (CONFORGE stochastic) per molecule — a musk olfactophore is materially more expensive than a muguet one for purely conformational reasons. Anything semiempirical or better is out of the screening regime entirely: a GFN2-xTB/CREST conformational search on a flexible 65-atom molecule takes **5–8 hours on 24 cores** ([TalTech HPC](https://docs.hpc.taltech.ee/chemistry/crest.html)), which is ~120–190 core-hours per molecule, roughly 10⁵× ETKDG.

Two practical constraints follow. Storage, not CPU, binds if you host your own searchable library: Pharmit's MolPort set is **104,330,134 conformations occupying 2.5 TB**, ~24 kB per conformer including index, with total hosted data >28 TB and preprocessing that "requires several days" for billion-conformer libraries ([Pharmit backend, JCIM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8049614/)) — my arithmetic puts a 10M-molecule odorant library at ~3.4 TB. And shape overlay, the natural companion method, costs "hundreds of molecules per CPU second" with ROCS ([OpenEye](https://www.eyesopen.com/rocs)), i.e. ~5–10 ms per molecule, with the open-source GPU implementation PAPER giving 1–2 orders of magnitude over a reference CPU implementation ([PAPER, J Comput Chem 2010](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/ihaque/papers/gpurocs.pdf)). Everything in this tier is a laptop job at 1k–100k scale.

## Docking 400 receptors is the compute cliff — and it barely predicts

One tier further down, costs jump five orders of magnitude and, critically, **predictive value does not follow**. GNINA 1.3, restricted to 4 CPU cores on a 100-complex PDBbind subset, took **23 s per complex for the default ensemble, 16 s for the fast CNN model and ~15 s for Vina scoring**, achieving PoseBusters top-1 ≤2 Å redocking of **67%**, cross-docking of only **40%**, and DUD-E median virtual-screening AUC **0.78** ([GNINA 1.3, J Cheminform 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11874439/)). Those are the generic drug-target numbers. The olfaction-specific numbers are far worse: the one published OR virtual-screening benchmark, on **OR5K1**, reports **ROC AUC 0.67 for homology models and 0.68 for AlphaFold2 models, with enrichment factors in the top 15% of only 0.11 and 0.24** ([bioRxiv 2022](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.06.01.494157v1.full)). **An EF below 1 means the top-ranked fraction is depleted of actives relative to random selection.** Single-receptor OR docking, on this evidence, is not merely weak — it is anti-predictive as a ranker.

The field's response has been to stop trusting any single pose and aggregate instead: a 2025 study built **~400–409 human ORs with AlphaFold2 and represented each odorant as a 409-dimensional docking-score vector**, comparing vectors statistically on the explicit rationale that this "compensates for inaccuracies in individual docking poses and affinities" ([ACS Omega 2025](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsomega.5c04324); its quantitative accuracy could not be recovered, so no correlation value should be attributed to it). That design choice sets the real unit cost: **~400 receptors × 23 s ≈ 2.5 CPU-hours per odorant** for a full docking-score vector (my arithmetic). Budgeting docking against "the muguet receptor" is budgeting for a method that does not work.

Above docking, everything is mechanism rather than screening. Co-folding with Boltz-2 costs **20–60 GPU-seconds per complex** (reconciling ~18 s, 27.8 s on an A6000, and 40–60 s in production deployment), which is 2–7 GPU-hours per odorant once multiplied by 400 receptors, with no OR-specific validation published. MD of a GPCR in a bilayer runs at **241.2 ns/day on an A100** for an 80,289-atom membrane benchmark ([NHR@FAU](https://hpc.fau.de/2022/02/10/gromacs-performance-on-different-gpu-types/)), so one 100 ns run is ~10 GPU-hours and ten candidates × three replicas is ~12.5 GPU-days. FEP sits at **~10 hours per ligand** with "each computed binding affinity might cost $10 or more in underlying cloud-compute costs" ([Rowan](https://www.rowansci.com/blog/how-to-predict-binding-affinity)) — congeneric series only, never a screening tier.

Laid out as a funnel for 10,000 novel candidates, with my arithmetic and the assumptions above:

| Tier | Method | Cost for the tier | Hardware | Predictive standing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | GNN odor model (POM/OpenPOM) + property filters | 10,000 × ~1 ms ≈ **10 s** | Mac | **Only method with prospective validation**: on 400 novel odorants the POM profile matched the trained panel mean better than the median panelist ([Science 2023](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ade4401)) |
| 1 | CONFORGE conformers + olfactophore fit (keep ~2,000) | 2,000 × 0.09 s ≈ **3 min** single-core | Mac | Interpretable and hypothesis-driven; **no published retrospective hit-rate or enrichment for any odor olfactophore** |
| 2 | Shape/electrostatic overlay vs known actives (~300) | 300 × ~10 ms ≈ **3 s** | Mac | Good analogue retrieval, no mechanism |
| 3 | Docking vs 409 AF2 OR models, GNINA fast | 300 × 409 × 64 core-s ≈ **2,180 core-hours** ≈ 17 h on one 128-core node | **Cluster** | AUC ~0.68, EF@15% < 1 per receptor; usable only as a vector |
| 4 | MD on survivors (10 × 3 replicas × 100 ns) | ~300 GPU-h ≈ **12.5 GPU-days** | **NVIDIA GPU** | Mechanism only |
| 5 | FEP on a congeneric series | ~10 GPU-h and **$10+ per ligand** | **NVIDIA GPU** | Congeneric series only |

**The compute cliff sits entirely between Tier 2 and Tier 3.** Everything through Tier 2 is a coffee break on a MacBook; Tier 3 is a cluster allocation. On costing Tier 4, using the sourced 2026 rates from the companion compute research — **RunPod $1.39–1.49/h for an A100 and $0.34/h for a community RTX 4090** ([RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)) — 300 GPU-hours is **~$420–450 on A100s or ~$100 on a community 4090** (my arithmetic; the 241 ns/day figure is A100-measured, and a 4090 is only reported as comparable in a vendor benchmark). For an LMU user, LRZ's AI Systems (4 × H100 and 8 × A100 nodes) are free after an access request ([LRZ](https://doku.lrz.de/lrz-ai-systems-11484278.html)). CPU-hour cloud pricing was not sourced in this research, so Tier 3 is given in core-hours only rather than dollars.

The Mac/GPU boundary is clean and worth stating explicitly. **Runs natively on Apple Silicon:** CDPKit/CONFORGE, all of RDKit (ETKDG, O3A, Pharm3D, fingerprints), Align-it, shape-it, OpenPOM inference, and AutoDock Vina/smina CPU docking at 1k–10k scale. **Requires CUDA:** GNINA's CNN scoring, every Vina-GPU variant, DiffDock, Boltz-2, AlphaFold, and GPU molecular dynamics — OpenMM has no native Metal platform, only a third-party plugin ([OpenMM issue #5397](https://github.com/openmm/openmm/issues/5397)). A laptop-only strategy is therefore not a compromise at Tiers 0–2; it is the whole of the method that currently has evidence behind it.

## Conclusion

The absence of an olfactophore database is not an oversight waiting to be fixed — it is the visible shadow of an industrial modelling practice that publishes conclusions and withholds coordinates. That asymmetry has a practical consequence worth naming: the *hardest* part of building a muguet or Calone olfactophore is not the modelling, which is an afternoon of CDPKit and Align-it, but assembling the training set with thresholds attached. OpenPOM gives binary perceptual labels; the published models were trained on measured odor thresholds, which is why they report correlations and cost functions rather than hit rates. A reconstruction built on binary GS-LF labels will be a *common-feature* model where the originals were *quantitative* ones, and it should be described as such.

The sharper conclusion concerns where to spend. The evidence points firmly against the intuitive escalation from pharmacophore fitting to docking to MD: the tiers get 10⁵× more expensive while the only olfaction-specific validation available shows single-receptor docking performing *worse than random* at enrichment, and the only method with a prospective novel-odorant validation is the cheapest one in the stack at roughly a millisecond per molecule. The rational pipeline is therefore inverted relative to drug discovery — a GNN odor model as the primary filter, a rebuilt olfactophore as the interpretable, mechanistically meaningful second stage, and docking reserved for the 409-dimensional *vector* representation rather than any single receptor hypothesis. And for the marine/Calone family specifically, the position is starker than for any other: no open label, no deorphanized receptor, no structure, and a single published inter-feature distance of 6.3 Å. Building that model from hand-curated GoodScents text would be, as far as this research can determine, the first machine-readable marine olfactophore anyone has made.
