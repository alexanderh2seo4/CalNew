# A laptop reaches the odor-potency accuracy ceiling

About fifteen small academic groups and a handful of companies build AI that predicts odor potency, and none of them is limited by compute. The field's best models are gradient-boosted trees or small neural networks on physicochemical descriptors, trained on 300–2,400 molecules. On random splits they reach about **R² 0.8 and an error of 0.8 log units (roughly 6×) for detection thresholds**, for example Jiangnan University's XGBoost on 1,003 water thresholds. For perceived intensity under controlled delivery the best result is **r ≈ 0.87**, from Monell and Cold Spring Harbor. Those random-split numbers already sit at the reproducibility limit of the labels, about 0.66 log units even within a single laboratory. On unseen chemical scaffolds, the only strict test (UCL with Metanovas) explains only about 40% of threshold variance. The only published compute disclosures are small: one GPU for 70–100 minutes per training run, and ten A100-class hours to pretrain a tri-modal foundation model. Industry, led by Osmo, discloses no potency architecture or compute at all. The practical conclusion is that a descriptor-first ensemble with censored, uncertainty-aware heads reaches every achievable accuracy tier on a laptop or for **under $100 of rented GPU**. The money and effort belong in labels: literature extraction costs roughly $150–1,100 in tokens for 50,000 papers, and human checking costs 150–400 hours. Above about 0.65 log units of error (4.5×), no amount of compute helps unless the labels themselves get better.

## Fifteen small labs set the benchmarks while industry stays silent

### Chinese food-science groups own threshold regression

The most active threshold-modelling cluster is in Chinese food science, and it works almost entirely with classical machine learning. **Jiangnan University (Wuxi), with the National University of Singapore**, published the best-documented model in 2026. It benchmarked nine learners on 121 descriptors for **1,003 aqueous thresholds** curated from van Gemert and post-2011 literature, and XGBoost won with **test R² 0.827, RMSE 0.83 log units** on a random 80/20 split. On 177 independently measured compounds, **87% of predictions fell within tenfold** ([Hou et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13558726/)). The group then screened 1,041 candidate flavor molecules and sent 12 to human sensory testing. Eight of those came in within tenfold of the prediction ([APFood](https://www.apfoodonline.com/industry/machine-learning-high-potency-aroma-compounds-food/)). **Hainan University with the Yazhouwan National Laboratory** reports the field's highest number: ECFP4 fingerprints with GBDT gave **R² 0.94, RMSE 0.44** on 716 water thresholds under a "scaffold-aware greedy" split ([Li et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12654368/)). That error is below the known reproducibility of threshold measurements, so it should be replicated before anyone treats it as a target.

**Hunan University with Case Western** opened a distinct line: predicting odor thresholds of *unidentified* water contaminants from MS² spectra instead of structures ([Huang et al., ES&T 2024](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.est.4c01763)). A 2026 SSRN preprint from Sun, Jia and Xi extends this to **1,741 organic and inorganic air thresholds** spanning 10⁻⁹–10⁸ mg/m³. It reports **structure-model test R² ≥ 0.65** and 0.81 accuracy for the MS² model ([SSRN 7070562](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7070562)); those figures come from secondary summaries and are unverified. **Xiamen University with DP Technology** built NOSE, a tri-modal contrastive model that links molecules to receptor sequences and odor text. It currently tops the 268-molecule Abraham threshold benchmark at **Pearson 0.84 ± 0.03** (R² ≈ 0.65) ([NOSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10452v2)). Beijing Technology and Business University produced the field's data-driven review: **848 compounds across 11 matrices from 146 studies**, with no machine learning at all ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)).

### Western groups focus on intensity, rigor and generalization

**Monell Chemical Senses Center, with Cold Spring Harbor, Haverford, Arizona State/Osmo and Ajinomoto**, built the most physically grounded intensity model. Thirty-six panelists rated 62 odorants at seven or more concentrations each, delivered in sealed Nalophan bags, plus 260 mixtures. Hill dose–response curves were fitted to those ratings, and a five-layer DNN on 127 Dragon descriptors was trained on them. It reached **r 0.87, RMSE 7.1 gLMS** under molecule-stratified cross-validation. A 10-feature transport-descriptor version performed identically, and predictions still correlated **r 0.62 with real GC-O data** ([Pellegrino & Mainland 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)). The group is publicly defending structure-to-odor prediction in an ongoing 2025–2026 exchange in *Chemical Senses* ([Mainland/Pellegrino](https://academic.oup.com/chemse/article-abstract/doi/10.1093/chemse/bjag003/8489487)). Mayhew and Mainland's earlier PNAS work showed that three transport criteria alone classify **>1,900 molecules as odorous or odorless** ([PNAS 2022](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2116576119)).

**Leipzig University (Fichtelmann & Westermayr)** has the best reproducibility package. They predicted **four-class ordinal odor strength for 2,393 molecules** and compared seven representations, including ChemBERTa-2, Chemprop and CheMeleon. Very similar molecules (Tanimoto ≥ 0.8) were forced into the same fold. They reached **72% accuracy**, with an RDKit-descriptor MLP + RF + XGBoost ensemble on top. Code and data are on GitHub and Zenodo under CC BY 3.0 ([RSC Advances 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)). **UCL with Metanovas Biotech (San Francisco)** published the only strict Bemis–Murcko scaffold-split threshold result. They trained a GINE/PNA graph network jointly on vapor pressure and 1,042 air and water thresholds. Threshold error stayed at **normalized MSE 0.605**, about three times worse than vapor pressure on the same backbone, and the multitask gain went to vapor pressure, not to thresholds ([Wu, Wang & Yu 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)). The **Porto/NTNU group (Nogueira, Ribeiro)** moved from a 45-molecule transfer-learning threshold study ([Oliveira 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10589844/)) to VIANA. VIANA fuses a GCN, Principal Odor Map embeddings and a Hill-law layer. It claims **R² 0.996** for intensity, a figure no psychophysics dataset can support, and its split protocol is not accessible ([VIANA](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01365)). The historical baseline comes from the **Mario Negri Institute (Toropov)**: r² 0.61–0.65 on 906 air thresholds with SMILES-fragment Monte Carlo models ([Mol. Divers. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11030-017-9800-5)).

Several influential groups are adjacent rather than central. The **University of Michigan** won DREAM 2017 intensity-related sub-challenges with a 100-tree random forest, and found that five descriptors perform nearly as well as all 4,884 ([Li, Guan et al.](https://academic.oup.com/gigascience/article/7/2/gix127/4750780)). A Michigan team under Tewari tied for first in the 2024/25 DREAM mixtures challenge ([U-M](https://lsa.umich.edu/stats/news-events/all-news/search-news/olfactory-mixtures-prediction-challenge-winners.html)). In that challenge the organizers' ensemble reached a median **RMSE 0.08 and Pearson 0.57** on perceptual distance ([DREAM mixtures](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.13.694160v1.full)). **Weizmann (Sobel)** and **Leibniz-LSB@TUM** mainly supply data: the Ravia intensity set and a database of more than 1,700 odorants ([LSB@TUM](https://www.leibniz-lsb.de/en/databases/leibniz-lsbtum-odorant-database/start/)). A 2026 *Cell Systems* paper (MolOR) uses GNN–protein-language cross-attention to predict receptor activation and then perception ([Cell Systems](https://www.cell.com/cell-systems/fulltext/S2405-4712%2826%2900193-6)). That receptor route does not yet produce potency numbers.

### Industry claims performance prediction but publishes no potency models

**Osmo**, the Google Research spin-out led by Alex Wiltschko, is the best-funded player. It has raised $130–133.5M, including a $70M Series B in February 2026, and has more than 100 staff ([Beauty Independent](https://www.beautyindependent.com/osmo-raises-70m-series-b-expand-ai-powered-fragrance-ingredient-design-manufacturing/)). It claims 5M human smell annotations and 3B molecules mapped ([Osmo](https://www.osmo.ai/about)). Its published core model is the Principal Odor Map: an MPNN with a 256-dimensional embedding, trained on about 5,000 molecules for odor *quality* ([Lee et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11898014/)). A linear head on frozen POM embeddings beat an SVM on Abraham thresholds, but no number was reported, and the authors note that concentration "is not explicitly included in the map" ([PMC11898014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11898014/)). Osmo's peer-reviewed intensity work runs through Monell co-authorships (Gerkin, Wiltschko), and it uses small descriptor DNNs, not large GNNs ([PMC12363845](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363845/)). Its only infrastructure disclosure is a 2023 Google Cloud case study: Vertex AI training and a 40-billion-molecule, ~15 TB BigQuery table, with **no accelerator counts** ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/how-osmo-is-bringing-ai-to-aromas)).

The large fragrance houses claim performance prediction without technical detail. IFF's Science of Performance combines "decades of measured data" with "proprietary AI" to manage scent intensity and release timing ([Personal Care Insights](https://www.personalcareinsights.com/news/iff-science-of-performance-fragrance-ai.html)). Givaudan's Carto optimizes performance using an "Odour Value Map" ([Givaudan](https://www.givaudan.com/media/media-releases/2019/givaudan-fragrances-launches-carto-its-artificial-intelligence-powered-tool)). Symrise's Philyra, built with IBM Research, draws on 3.5M formulas ([Symrise](https://www.symrise.com/scent-and-care/competence-platforms/philyra/)). dsm-firmenich's emotiOn targets emotion, not potency ([dsm-firmenich](https://our-company.dsm-firmenich.com/en/our-company/news/trade-news/2025/dsm-firmenich-unveils-emotion-social-connection.html)). The only published industrial potency method is **Kao's dose–response database**: 314 perfumery materials rated by 18 experts. It uses curve fitting, not deep learning, and reaches **RMSE 6.22** for single components ([Wakayama et al. 2019](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.iecr.9b01225)). Among startups, NotCo's encoder–decoder "Generative Aroma Transformer" targets formulation ([The Spoon](https://thespoon.tech/notco-has-created-a-generative-ai-for-flavor-and-fragrance-that-can-create-unique-formulations-with-text-prompts/)), and Aromyx's receptor biochip produces EC50-style data with no public link to perceptual thresholds ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2017/12/21/The-Google-Maps-for-taste-and-scent-Aromyx-s-bio-chip-sensor-could-fuel-future-reformulation-it-says/)). The identifiable ML olfaction patents are Google-origin, now Osmo's, and cover odor quality, with intensity only as an optional output ([US20220139504](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20220139504)). The most likely explanation is that the houses keep potency knowledge as trade-secret lookup tables of odor values rather than as learned models, but that is an inference.

## Descriptors plus boosted trees still match every deep architecture on potency

The architecture evidence splits sharply by task. On odor *quality*, which has about 5,000 molecules and 138 correlated labels, deep graph models clearly win. The GraphNOSE graph transformer (four GPS layers, about 314K parameters) reached **89.2% AUROC against OpenPOM's 87.1%, MoLFormer's 85.0% and ChemBERTa's 84.8%**, and held its lead out of distribution and with 30–50% of the training data ([GraphNOSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05694)). On *potency*, which is a single noisy scalar, no deep model has beaten a descriptor model beyond noise. Leipzig's four best strength models were **statistically indistinguishable**, and descriptor ensembles beat Chemprop, CheMeleon and ChemBERTa-2 ([PMC13182554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13182554/)). MoLFormer showed "no clear incremental predictive value" over an RDKit-plus-Morgan baseline on Keller intensity ([arXiv 2607.24848](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24848)). The one clear foundation-model win comes from NOSE on the Abraham thresholds: **Pearson 0.84 versus 0.78 for Uni-Mol, 0.72 for GIN and 0.60 for Morgan fingerprints**. That result used a random split on 268 molecules ([NOSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10452v2)).

General chemistry benchmarks explain the pattern. Across 26 ADME, toxicity and activity endpoints, **RF or ExtraTrees on ECFP4 or RDKit descriptors won 116 of 156 comparisons**, against 25 for GNNs, 12 for pretrained sequence models and 3 for LLM-based SAR. Classical ML dominated at every training size of 1,000 compounds or more. Under structure-separated splits, however, GNNs lost less accuracy than trees ([Guo et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26498v2)). CheMeleon shows what makes a deep model competitive at small data. The architecture is the same D-MPNN as vanilla Chemprop, but it is **pretrained on 1,613 computed descriptors across 1M molecules**. That lifts the Polaris win rate from 32% to **75%, above random forest's 68%** ([CheMeleon](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15792)). The lever is the pretraining target, not scale. Every interpretable potency model points to the same physics: **size, polarity, volatility and hydrophobicity**. Abraham's solvation equation explains ~77% of threshold variance from a handful of descriptors ([Abraham 2002](https://academic.oup.com/chemse/article/27/2/95/351961)), and Monell's 10-feature transport model matches its 127-feature model.

The split column matters more than the model column. The field uses four different strictness levels under similar names, and only Wu's is the standard MoleculeNet-style scaffold protocol. Headline R² values of 0.83–0.94 describe interpolation within one curated matrix. Values of about 0.61–0.65 on held-out or harder data (Toropov, NOSE, Sun) and roughly 0.4 on strict scaffolds (Wu) describe what a model does on genuinely new chemistry. Intensity shows a second gap. Under controlled delivery, structure explains most of perceived intensity (r 0.87 against a panel test–retest of 0.97). On legacy public datasets with jar or paraffin-oil delivery, even a 2026 foundation model manages only r 0.42–0.49 ([NOSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10452v2)). The difference comes from the stimulus data, not the model.

## Five accuracy tiers show where compute stops helping

"Reasonable accuracy" needs a noise-aware definition, because threshold labels have a hard floor. Abraham estimated the self-consistency of Nagata's single-lab threshold series at **0.66 log units** ([Abraham 2012](https://academic.oup.com/chemse/article/37/3/207/325917)). Between laboratories, spreads reach two to four orders of magnitude, driven by method, panel and purity ([Yang et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13420774/)). The noise sets a ceiling. If labels carry noise σ, test RMSE cannot fall below about σ, and the maximum R² is 1 − σ²/Var(y). Hou's and Li's reported R²/RMSE pairs both imply a label standard deviation of about 1.8–2.0 log units (my arithmetic). With σ ≈ 0.66–0.8, the random-split R² ceiling is therefore roughly **0.80–0.89**, and Hou's 0.83 sits inside that band. The tiers below use log₁₀ RMSE as the primary metric; the fold factor is 10^RMSE. The random-split R² values assume a label SD of about 1.9 log, and the scaffold column is my proposed target for the same tier on unseen chemotypes.

| Tier | Threshold RMSE (fold error) | R² random / scaffold | Who reaches it today | What it takes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T0 Screening | ≤ 1.3 (~20×) | ≥ 0.5 / ≥ 0.25 | Abraham LSER, Toropov | A few hundred labels, linear or tree model, CPU minutes |
| T1 Triage | ≤ 1.0 (10×) | ≥ 0.7 / ≥ 0.4 | NOSE on Abraham-268; Wu on scaffolds (borderline) | ~1,000 labels in one medium; descriptors + GBDT; laptop, <1 h |
| T2 Published SOTA | ≤ 0.85 (~7×) | ≈ 0.8 / ≈ 0.5 | Hou 2026 (random split only) | ~1,000 harmonized labels; tuned GBDT or descriptor ensemble; laptop, hours |
| T3 Noise floor | ≈ 0.65 (4.5×) | ≈ 0.88 / ≥ 0.6 | Nobody verified (Li's 0.44 is unreplicated) | Single-protocol or consensus-harmonized labels; same compute as T2 |
| T4 Within 3× | ≤ 0.48 (3×) | ≥ 0.93 / ≥ 0.75 | Unreachable on single-measurement labels | Replicate-averaged labels (k ≈ 3 gives σ ≈ 0.4); a measurement programme, not a model |

The same logic applies to other potency targets. For perceived intensity, r ≥ 0.6 on real-world data counts as useful (Monell's GC-O transfer reached 0.62), and r ≈ 0.85–0.87 is the controlled-delivery state of the art. Glass-jar data cap out lower because their test–retest r is only 0.72–0.81 ([Andres et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/chemse/bjag022)). For ordinal strength, 72% four-class accuracy is the current bar. For odorous-versus-odorless, logistic regression already reaches **ROC AUC 0.97** on 4,983 molecules ([ChemRxiv 2024](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/6726033df9980725cfdb7843/original/odor-classification-exploring-feature-performance-and-imbalanced-data-learning-techniques.pdf)). That problem is effectively solved.

The scaling literature confirms that moving up a tier is a data problem. Molecular-property error falls as a power law in training-set size, with exponents around −0.5 ([Hestness et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.00409)). Under that law, cutting scaffold-split RMSE from 0.8 to 0.6 needs about **1.8× more molecules**, and the compute for each fit stays at minutes (my arithmetic). Model scale buys little. Chemical language models improve pretraining loss only about 11% per doubling of model size ([Frey et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-023-00740-3)). A 2026 survey found that growing property-prediction models beyond ~15M parameters hurt average performance ([arXiv 2601.22757](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22757v1)). Molecular foundation models also often fail to beat random forests on fingerprints in low-data regimes ([arXiv 2509.00614](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.00614)). More data helps mainly on scaffold splits. On random splits, T2 is already at the ceiling, so extra molecules help only if they also carry cleaner labels.

## Under $100 of GPU trains the model; the money goes to labels

### Disclosed compute is tiny, and pretraining is the only expensive item

Only two potency papers disclose compute, and both are small. NOSE pretrained on a **single A800-80G for about 10 hours** in bfloat16. Its downstream threshold and intensity runs took **2–3 minutes each**, and a full sweep of 480 configurations × 3 seeds took 1–3 days per task ([NOSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10452v2)). At September 2026 RunPod rates of **$1.39–1.49/h for an A100** and **$0.34/h for a community RTX 4090** ([RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)), that is about $15 of pretraining and $8–107 per task sweep (my arithmetic). Wu, Wang and Yu report **70–100 minutes per scaffold-split run on one GPU** ([arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)). If each of the 3 partitions × 5 seeds is a separate run, the whole protocol is about 18–25 GPU-hours, or $6–35 (my arithmetic). The classical papers from Jiangnan, Hainan and Mario Negri disclose only library versions, because XGBoost on 1,000 × 121 matrices trains in seconds.

Measured timings from adjacent work fill in the rest. On one laptop with a Quadro RTX 4000, Chemprop trained a ~800-molecule dataset in **16 minutes 55 seconds**. A descriptor MLP (fastprop) did the same in **under 2 minutes**, most of it descriptor calculation ([fastprop](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02058v3)). Chemprop's authors say ~1,000-molecule datasets train "within a few minutes on a standard laptop with CPUs only" ([Chemprop v2](https://chemrxiv.org/doi/pdf/10.26434/chemrxiv-2025-4p1nr)). Those fastprop timings imply about 0.12 s per molecule for Mordred descriptors, or roughly 10 minutes for 5,000 molecules (my estimate). The flagship POM paper's cost was almost entirely hyperparameter search: **1,000 Vizier trials × 5 folds** ([Lee et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11898014/)). I reconstruct that at roughly 250–400 GPU-hours, about $350–800 at 2026 rates; the hardware is undisclosed. Money only becomes significant when a foundation model is pretrained from scratch:

| ChemBERTa-2 | ~5 days on 4 × T4 for 77M compounds ([arXiv 2209.01712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.01712)) | 480 T4-h ≈ $145–240 |

| CheMeleon (12.9M params) | 1M molecules, hardware undisclosed ([arXiv 2506.15792](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15792)) | Low tens of A100-h, ≈ $30–100 (estimate) |

None of these needs to be repeated, because MoLFormer, ChemBERTa, Uni-Mol and CheMeleon checkpoints are open. CheMeleon fine-tunes with one Chemprop flag (`--from-foundation CheMeleon`) ([CheMeleon repo](https://github.com/JacksonBurns/chemeleon)).

### Four hardware budgets cover every tier

A MacBook covers T0 through T3 at no marginal cost. That includes RDKit/Mordred features, XGBoost or RF with a 200-trial Optuna search and 5-fold CV (about 35 minutes by my estimate), Chemprop on CPU, frozen-embedding heads, and a full learning-curve study overnight. On Apple Silicon, trees and descriptors are CPU-bound anyway, and message-passing operations are the class most likely to hit MPS gaps, so Chemprop should simply run on CPU. A **single consumer GPU** adds speed and full fine-tuning of the open checkpoints. A generous 50-GPU-hour campaign covering all model classes, sweeps and ensembles costs **$17 on a community RTX 4090 or $50 on an RTX 5090** ([RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)). A **single A100/H100** is needed only for local LLM extraction, full LLM fine-tunes or reproducing CheMeleon-style pretraining. A 20-hour job costs $28–54 at $1.39–2.69/h. For an LMU user the same work is free on the LRZ AI Systems (4 × H100 and 8 × A100 nodes) after an access request ([LRZ](https://doku.lrz.de/lrz-ai-systems-11484278.html)). A **cluster** is justified only for pretraining at MoLFormer or Uni-Mol2 scale, which the scaling evidence argues against.

### Reconciling the extraction bill: batch pricing halves the earlier placeholders

Since data limits accuracy, the largest defensible compute spend is building labels from the literature. Parsing is cheap. MinerU 2.5 runs at about 2.1 pages/s on an A100, so 500,000–600,000 pages (50,000 papers) take about **66–79 A100-hours, or $90–110** ([MinerU 2.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.22186); [Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/self-host-document-intelligence-docling-marker-mineru-rag-guide/)). Marker 2's vendor-reported fast mode (7.4 pages/s on a B200) needs about 19 GPU-hours ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/07/24/datalab-marker-v2-vs-mineru-docling-and-liteparse-benchmark-breakdown/)).

Earlier API estimates of about $263 for a "small" model and $3,150 for a "large" one used unverified placeholder prices. Checked against September 2026 list prices, those placeholders turn out to equal Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite ($0.25/$1.50 per million tokens) and Claude Sonnet 4.6 ($3/$15) at *standard* rates. They left out the **50% batch discount** that both vendors offer for asynchronous jobs ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [BenchLM Claude pricing](https://benchlm.ai/anthropic/api-pricing)). Two refinements narrow the range. A cheap pre-filter (caption regex, then a Flash-Lite classifier at ~$0.0003 per paper) that passes only 20–40% of papers cuts Sonnet-class spend on 50,000 papers to **$215–430**. Dual-model verification (Sonnet 5 plus Gemini 3.1 Pro, both batched) roughly doubles the unfiltered cost to about $2,225. The reconciled answer is therefore **about $150–1,100 in tokens for a realistic 50,000-paper run**, or about a fifth of that for a focused 10,000-paper run. Local open-weight models land in the same range as cheap APIs, so the choice should rest on extraction quality and data governance. The binding cost is people: roughly **150–400 hours of human checking** plus 4–8 person-weeks of engineering. Unit and medium errors (µg/L versus µg/m³, air versus water) would add systematic error larger than the label noise itself. Extraction precision near 90% is typical only with verification prompts ([ChatExtract, Nat Commun 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-45914-8)).

## A descriptor-first ensemble with censored heads is the build to copy

The recommended architecture follows directly from the head-to-head results. Its input layer concatenates three blocks. The first is RDKit or Mordred descriptors. The second is explicit transport terms: logP, predicted vapor pressure, hydrogen-bond acidity and basicity, McGowan volume and TPSA. These are the features that Abraham's equation, Monell's 10-feature model and every SHAP analysis identify. The third is **frozen** pretrained embeddings: CheMeleon for general chemistry and a 256-d OpenPOM or NOSE/Uni-Mol vector for olfactory character. Both successful threshold-transfer studies froze the graph model and put a tiny head on top, rather than fine-tuning ([POM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11898014/); [Oliveira 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10589844/)). On top sits a small MLP with at most two hidden layers and separate heads per measurement type. Air and water thresholds each get a heteroscedastic Gaussian head trained with a **Tobit likelihood**, so that ">" and "<" values count as constraints rather than being discarded; the Tobit adaptation for censored drug-discovery labels, with a public implementation in UQ4DD, supplies this ([arXiv 2409.04313](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04313); [UQ4DD](https://github.com/MolecularAI/uq4dd)). Strength classes get an ordinal CORAL head scored by macro-MSE, as in Leipzig's study. Intensity gets a **Hill-curve output layer**, the inductive bias behind both Monell's augmentation and VIANA.

The ensemble should have about ten members, mixing seeds and families. It should include XGBoost and RF on descriptors, the MLP heads, and a CheMeleon-initialized D-MPNN. The GNN stays in even when it loses on random splits, because GNNs degrade less on new scaffolds ([Guo et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26498v2)). Ensemble and evidential uncertainties are systematically miscalibrated, so post-hoc calibration on held-out data is mandatory ([Garain et al. 2026](https://chemistry-europe.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/chem.202503299?af=R)).

Three choices deserve explicit hedging. First, multitask co-training with vapor pressure is plausible but unproven for thresholds: in the only test, the gain went to vapor pressure and threshold error did not improve ([arXiv 2601.16426](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16426)). Predicted vapor pressure is therefore safer as an input feature than as a co-trained task. Co-training with the 138 GS-LF quality labels is the experiment most likely to pay off, because that correlated multi-label structure is what lets GNNs win the quality task. Second, 3D and equivariant models are worth adding only if enantiomer-resolved thresholds become a deliberate target. 2D graphs and SMILES cannot distinguish enantiomers, as GraphNOSE's authors acknowledge ([GraphNOSE](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05694)). Third, end-to-end SMILES transformers, LLM few-shot prediction and new large pretraining runs are poor bets: they rank last in the olfaction and general benchmarks.

Validation should copy Wu's protocol: Bemis–Murcko 80/10/10 across three partitions × five seeds. It should add a source-held-out split and a homologous-series holdout, and report the share of predictions within 3× and within 10× next to RMSE. Running the whole stack, including learning curves and ablations, fits in one laptop week plus **under 50 rented GPU-hours**.

## Conclusion

The field's accuracy problem is misdiagnosed as a modelling problem. The random-split threshold numbers that dominate the literature already equal the reproducibility of a single laboratory, so the architecture race among XGBoost, GNNs and tri-modal foundation models is being run inside the label noise. Leipzig's finding that its top four strength models are statistically tied, and UCL's finding that a strict scaffold split explains only ~40% of threshold variance, are the two results that should steer investment. The frontier is generalization to new chemistry and labels cleaner than 0.66 log units, and neither responds to FLOPs. One implication is that the most valuable open contribution would be a shared benchmark: one harmonized, provenance-tagged threshold set with fixed scaffold splits and fold-error metrics. Such a benchmark would immediately show whether Li's R² 0.94 or VIANA's R² 0.996 survives.

For a new team, the budget inverts the usual AI assumption. Compute for the model is a rounding error, at €0 on a laptop, under $100 in the cloud, or free on LRZ. Compute for the data, meaning parsing and LLM extraction, is a few hundred to about a thousand dollars. Human curation, and eventually single-protocol measurement of scaffold-diverse compounds with enantiomer pairs, is where both the cost and the accuracy gains lie. Osmo's reliance on small Monell psychophysics datasets for its own published intensity work suggests that even the best-funded player faces the same constraint.
